In short

  • The best senior dog photoshoot happens at home or somewhere familiar, in short sessions, with the dog lying where they like to lie.
  • Get down to their eye level and use window light or the hour before sunset. Skip the flash.
  • Close-ups of the gray muzzle, paws, whiskers and eyes are the photos people say they wish they had.
  • Put yourself in the frame, and hand the phone to someone else for a few minutes.
  • Add sound and short videos: the greeting, the tail, their breathing. Photos alone miss half of a dog.

The best senior dog photoshoot ideas ask the dog to do almost nothing. Photograph them where they already like to lie, at their eye level, in window light or the hour before sunset, in short sessions on non-slip ground. Get close-ups of the gray muzzle and paws, get yourself in the frame, and record a few sounds and short videos while you are at it.

Before you start: keep it short and comfortable

An old dog is the model, so the session runs on their terms. PDSA, the UK vet charity, notes that hard floors and the cold can be painful for older joints, and suggests shorter, more frequent activity rather than long sessions. For a photoshoot that means:

  • Shoot on a rug, a bed or grass, never on tiles or a slippery floor.
  • Keep each session to a few minutes. Spread the ideas over several days.
  • Bring their bed or blanket outdoors so they have somewhere soft to lie.
  • Stop when they lie down and look away, or their tail drops. That is the end of the session, not a problem to fix.
  • Keep treats to the ones they already eat. Ask your vet if they are on a special diet.

If your dog's hearing or sight has faded, make sure they can see you coming. Fear Free's guide for senior dogs, reviewed by a veterinary behaviorist, warns that a hearing-impaired dog approached from behind can be startled to the point of panic. Approach from the front and move slowly.

Senior dog photoshoot ideas at home

Home is where an old dog is most relaxed, and relaxed is what looks good in a photo. Most of these need no posing at all.

  1. The favorite sleeping spot, twice. Once with them in it, as you find them. Once empty, with the dent still in the cushion. The empty one is the strange one to look at later.
  2. The sun patch. Many old dogs follow a square of sunlight across the floor. Photograph them in it from floor level.
  3. The doorway wait: where they wait for you, or for dinner.
  4. The favorite toy as it is now: missing eye, torn seam. Then one of them holding it.
  5. Collar and tag, laid flat on a plain surface in daylight, close enough to read. Cover your phone number before sharing it anywhere.
  6. An old photo, recreated. Find one from their first months, then stand in the same spot at the same angle. Put the two side by side.

These are drawn from the Capture and Preserve parts of our book, One Day, You'll Miss This: activity 25 photographs the sleeping spot both ways, activity 45 the favorite toy, activity 14 recreates an old memory.

Close-ups: gray muzzle, paws and details

Most dog photos are taken from four feet away. The details are what you forget first, because the camera was never pointed at them. Take a set of nine or ten close-ups, none of them a portrait:

  • the nose, wet and textured
  • the gray coming in around the muzzle and eyebrows
  • the whiskers (they need side light to show at all)
  • the eyes, close enough to see the color
  • the ears, inside and out
  • the paws, pads up if they'll let you, one resting in your hand for scale
  • the swirl in the coat on the shoulder or chest

Only handle their feet if they are happy with it. If not, photograph the paws where they lie. Keep the set together in one folder. Separately they are fragments. Together they are the closest thing to a portrait you will take.

Dog photoshoot ideas with owner

The photo most owners don't have is one with both of them in it. You can't hold the camera and be in the picture, so hand the phone to someone else.

  • Sit on the floor with them. Get to their level instead of bending over.
  • Your hand on their back, or their chin on your knee.
  • The ear scratch, in the voice you actually use.
  • Their favorite person, if that person is not you. Ask whoever is filming to wait until neither of you is looking at the camera.

If there is nobody to help, prop the phone on a shelf and use the timer, or film a minute of video and take stills from it later.

Outdoor senior dog photoshoot ideas

Pick somewhere they know: the end of the usual walk, a quiet corner of the park, your own back step. A new place is fun for a young dog. An old dog with poor sight can find it unsettling.

Go in the hour before sunset and put the sun behind them. Low light comes through the edge of a coat and lifts the outline of the dog away from the background. You will get one good frame in ten, which is normal. Bring a blanket for them to lie on and water, and skip it on hot days.

Add sound and video

A photo shows what a dog looked like. It does not keep their footsteps on the kitchen floor, the thump of the tail, or the sigh when they lie down. Each of them takes seconds to record on a phone, and you are already sitting on the floor with the dog.

While you are set up anyway:

  • Film the tail at floor level, sound on. Half of a tail is the noise of it hitting furniture.
  • Film the greeting when you come home, from your side of the door.
  • Record two minutes of their breathing while they sleep, television off.
  • Film one completely ordinary minute in the kitchen, without calling their name.

Should you hire a senior dog photographer?

A professional is worth it for big prints, a family group, or if you simply want to be in the photos without thinking about it. Choose someone who has photographed old dogs before, and tell them about joints, hearing, sight and how long your dog can stay comfortable. Ask them to come to your home or a place your dog knows.

The everyday photos are still yours to take, on an ordinary Tuesday, and a photographer can't stand in for those. When you have them, pick the best twenty-five and keep them together. Questions people ask

How long should a senior dog photoshoot be?

Short. Plan several sessions of a few minutes rather than one long one, and stop when the dog lies down, looks away or loses interest. For an old dog, three good frames on three different days beat an hour of forced poses.

What should I wear for a photoshoot with my senior dog?

Plain clothes in a color that contrasts with the coat: darker for a pale dog, lighter for a dark dog. Avoid busy patterns and anything that sheds fluff, and wear something you can sit on the floor in.

Is flash bad for old dogs?

We suggest skipping it. A sudden flash can startle any dog, and dogs whose sight or hearing has faded are easier to startle. Window light or soft evening light looks better on a gray coat anyway.

Should I hire a senior dog photographer?

If you want large prints or photos of the whole family together, a photographer who works with older dogs is worth it. Tell them about your dog's joints, hearing and stamina before booking. Many of the best photos, though, are the everyday ones only you can take.
