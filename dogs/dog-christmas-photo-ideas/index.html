In short

  • The best dog Christmas photos usually need no costume: your dog by the tree, in their usual spot, or with their person.
  • Take last year's Christmas photo again, same spot and same angle, and put the two side by side.
  • Keep sessions to a few minutes and stop when your dog yawns, licks their lips, turns their head away or tucks their tail.
  • Leave mistletoe, holly, tinsel, snow globes, candles and food off the set; the ASPCA lists them as holiday hazards.
  • For an older dog, shoot on a non-slip rug, at floor level, in daylight, with no posing.

The easiest dog Christmas photos need no costume: your dog lying by the tree with the lights blurred behind them, asleep in their usual spot with the decorations up, or sitting with the person they love most. Shoot in daylight at their eye level, keep each go to a few minutes, and take last year's photo again in the same place.

Below are ideas sorted by how much they ask of your dog, a list of props to keep off the set, and what to do differently with an older dog.

Dog Christmas picture ideas at home

These work in an ordinary living room and need nothing you don't already have once the tree is up.

  • The tree at their level. Put the phone on the floor, your dog between you and the tree, and let the lights go soft in the background. Portrait mode on most phones does this for you.
  • The usual spot, decorated. Photograph your dog where they already lie every evening, with a string of lights or the tree just in the frame. It will look like your house, which is the point.
  • Christmas morning, as it happens. Your dog sniffing the paper, carrying off a box, asleep among the mess. Stand back and keep shooting.
  • Their stocking. Hang it low, photograph your dog checking it out. Use their usual chew or toy inside, not human treats.
  • The window. A dog watching snow or a dark street with fairy lights reflected in the glass.
  • Asleep under the tree. Some dogs end up there anyway. Take the photo without waking them.

For each of these, you choose the place and wait for the dog, instead of putting the dog in a place and asking them to stay.

Take last year's photo again

This is the idea we would put first. Activity 14 in our book, One Day, You'll Miss This, is called Recreate an Old Memory: find an old photograph and take it again. Same place, same angle, same people in the frame if they are still nearby. Then put the two side by side.

At Christmas it is easy, because the setting comes back every year. Find your dog's first Christmas photo with you, or simply last year's. Stand where you stood, at the same height, with the tree in roughly the same corner. Everything else will have changed: the room, the decorations, the phone, the gray on your dog's face. The arrangement is what stays.

If you do it every December, the pairs become a row. A row of Christmases with the same dog in the same corner is a better card than any prop.

Dog picture ideas for Christmas cards

A card photo has a job: it has to read at small size and leave room for words. A few things help.

  1. Decide the shape first. Most printed cards are portrait or square. Turn the phone to match before you start, and leave empty space above or beside your dog for the greeting.
  2. Keep the background plain. A dark wall or a blurred tree works better than a full room.
  3. Light the face. Put your dog side-on to a window. Turn the flash off; daylight gives softer, truer color.
  4. Fill the frame with the dog. A small dog in a big room disappears on a card.
  5. Take fifty frames, not five. Burst mode, then pick one.
  6. Choose a photo that looks like your dog, not like a stock image. The slightly crooked ear is what people recognize.

For a family card, sit on the floor with your dog rather than lifting them into your lap, and ask someone else to take it. Our book's activity Film Them With Their Favorite Person makes the same point about video: hand the phone over and do what you normally do with your dog.

Christmas props: what is safe to use

Many prop lists online include things that are hazards. The ASPCA's holiday safety page lists the main ones, and its poison control page lists the foods. We put them side by side here.

Keep the tree secured so it can't tip, and give your dog a quiet room to go to when the house fills up, as the ASPCA suggests. If your dog eats something on this list, call your vet.

Dog Christmas photo ideas without a costume

Hats and antlers turn up in every list. Some dogs tolerate them for a few seconds; plenty don't. If you want to try, put the hat on once, take the photo, and take it off. Or skip it: a red blanket, a plaid scarf you are wearing, or the tree behind them says Christmas just as clearly.

The RSPCA describes what a worried dog looks like. Stop the session when you see any of these:

  • Yawning when they are not tired.
  • Licking their lips.
  • Turning their head away or avoiding your eyes.
  • Ears back, tail tucked, body or head held low.
  • Raising a front paw.

These are easy to miss when you are looking at the screen, so glance over the top of the phone now and then. A photo of a dog who wants to leave usually shows it anyway.

Senior dog Christmas photo

An older dog may not sit or stay for long, and hard floors can make getting up difficult. The PDSA notes that laminate and tiles can be slippery for older pets and suggests non-slip rugs, and that cold, hard floors can be painful for older joints. So set the shot up around where your dog likes to lie:

  • Put a non-slip rug or their bed by the tree, and let them settle there on their own.
  • Bring the camera down to them. Don't lift them onto furniture for the shot.
  • Shoot in the morning or early afternoon, when the window light is best and many old dogs are rested.
  • Go for close-ups: the gray muzzle with the lights blurred behind, a paw on a red blanket.
  • Take two or three short goes on different days rather than one long one.

Our guide to senior dog photoshoot ideas has more ideas sorted by how much the dog has to move. If you are making a list of things to do with your old dog this winter, a Christmas photo by the tree is a good entry on a senior dog bucket list.

Funny dog Christmas photo ideas

Funny photos are rarely staged. They are what happens before and after the staged one:

  • The family card outtakes, where everyone is looking at the camera except the dog.
  • Your dog asleep in the middle of the wrapping-paper mess.
  • A dog inspecting a parcel with their name on it, with total seriousness.
  • The face when the doorbell goes on Christmas Eve.
  • Your dog in the one spot by the radiator, ignoring the whole holiday.

Keep the phone in your pocket on the day and keep shooting after the "proper" photo is done.

Dog and kid Christmas photo ideas

Children and dogs make great photos when the child is beside the dog, not wrapped around them. Have the child sit on the floor next to the dog, reading, opening a present or sharing a blanket, and let the dog choose how close to be. An adult stays right there. If the dog shows any of the signs above, end it.

What to do with the photos

Pick one for the card, then save the rest somewhere you will find them next December, ready to take again. If you are building a book of your dog's life, our dog memory book ideas suggest a short list of 25 photos, and a Christmas one usually earns its place.

Questions people ask

How do I get a good Christmas photo of my dog?

Shoot in daylight near a window, get down to your dog's eye level, and use the tree or lights as a blurred background behind them. Keep it to a few minutes, take lots of frames, and stop as soon as your dog looks worried or bored.

Is it OK to put a Santa hat on my dog?

Some dogs ignore a light hat for a few seconds; many don't like it. Try it once, for a moment. If your dog freezes, lowers their head, licks their lips, yawns or paws at it, take it off. Most of the good ideas on this page need no costume at all.

What Christmas props are dangerous for dogs?

The ASPCA lists mistletoe and holly, poinsettias, tinsel, breakable ornaments, snow globes (they contain ethylene glycol), wires and batteries, and unattended candles. Keep food out of the set too: chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic and anything with xylitol are dangerous for dogs. If your dog eats something, call your vet.

How do I take a Christmas card photo of my senior dog?

Let them lie where they are comfortable, on a non-slip rug, and bring the camera down to them rather than lifting or posing them. Use window light, skip props, and take the shot in two or three short goes rather than one long session.

What are some funny dog Christmas photo ideas?

The funny photos usually happen on their own: your dog asleep under the tree, inspecting a parcel addressed to them, ignoring the camera completely, or the outtakes from a family photo. Keep the camera ready on the day and keep shooting after the 'proper' photo.
