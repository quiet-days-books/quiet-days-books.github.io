In short

  • There is no single right number. Chewy's vets say there is no set formula; breed and health change the answer for each dog.
  • Little and often works best for most older dogs, says PDSA: shorter routes, plenty of rest stops, and some time outdoors every day.
  • Judge the amount by your dog: stiffness after a walk, lagging behind or lying down mean it was too much, so shorten the next one.
  • Walk in the cooler parts of the day in summer, choose softer ground like grass, and avoid fast starts, jumping and running on hard surfaces.
  • Ask your vet before changing your dog's exercise, and stop and call them if your dog limps, pants heavily or seems in pain.

Walk a senior dog little and often: shorter routes, more rest stops, cooler hours, and some time outdoors every day. How long is right depends on the dog. Use what they manage comfortably now as the starting point, watch how they are afterwards, and ask your vet before you change their routine.

That is less tidy than a number, and for a good reason. A fit twelve-year-old terrier and a stiff nine-year-old giant breed are both "senior", and they need very different walks.

How much exercise does a senior dog need?

There is no single answer, and the better sources say so plainly. Chewy's senior exercise guide states there is no set formula for how much is enough, and names breed and health as the two main things that change it: athletic breeds often stay keen, mellower ones slow down sooner, and illness, injury or arthritis can limit any dog.

Some guides do give a range. PetMD's article, written by a vet, suggests at least 30 to 60 minutes of exercise a day for many senior dogs, two 30-minute walks for dogs who are up for it, and around 20 minutes a day for dogs with mobility problems or medical conditions. Treat those as a rough outline from one vet's guide. Your own dog, and your own vet, set the real figure.

What every source agrees on is the shape:

  • Some activity every day. PDSA says older dogs still need the chance to get outdoors daily, even when longer walks are out of reach.
  • Little and often. PDSA's reasoning is simple: joints get stiffer when they have not been used for a while.
  • Gentler, not none. The AKC calls walking an excellent low-impact exercise, and Chewy's guide keeps it at the top of its list for older dogs.

If you are not sure whether your dog counts as a senior yet, our guide to when a dog is considered a senior explains how vets think about age.

How to find the right amount for your dog

Since the number is personal, the most useful thing is a way to find it. Watch three moments.

When What to look for What it tells you
During the walk Keeping pace, sniffing, interested; or lagging, stopping, lying down PDSA says a dog who lies down on a walk needs to rest
Just after Settling normally; or stiff, struggling to get up, panting hard The AKC says stiffness after a walk means take a step back
Next morning Moving as usual; or slow, sore, reluctant to go out A delayed sign the walk was too long

Start with the walk your dog does comfortably now. If they come home content and move normally the next day, that amount is fine. If they show any of the signs in the right-hand column, make the next walk shorter, or split it into two. Chewy's guide quotes a vet making the same point: dogs tell you how much they want through how they behave after walks.

A short note on paper or your phone for a week, with the route, roughly how long it took and how your dog was the next day, makes the pattern much easier to see. It is also useful to show your vet.

Walking a senior dog: what to change

The walk itself changes shape as a dog ages. Most of these come straight from the vet and charity guides.

  1. Shorter, closer routes. PDSA suggests keeping to a fairly short route in case your dog gets tired, with lots of rest stops.
  2. Slower pace. Let your dog set it, and expect to go much more slowly than you used to.
  3. Softer ground. The AKC calls grass ideal and warns that asphalt and gravel can be hard on paw pads, especially in warm weather.
  4. No sudden bursts. Chewy advises avoiding jumping for balls, fast starts and stops, and fast running on hard surfaces like concrete.
  5. A comfortable harness, which Chewy recommends for walking older dogs.
  6. More time to sniff. Sniffing is slow and low-impact, and it lets the dog set the pace.

Point 6 is where our book One Day, You'll Miss This starts. Its first activity, "Let Them Choose the Walk", asks you to keep deciding what is safe while your dog decides everything else: which way at each corner, and how long to stay when they stop to smell something. You cover less ground and it takes longer. Ten minutes is enough. For an older dog, that slow, chosen walk often suits the body better than the brisk loop you used to do.

Weather and time of day

Older dogs handle extremes less well. PDSA says they do not cope as well with very high or low temperatures, and the AKC says the same about heat and cold.

In practice:

  • In summer, walk in the cooler parts of the day, early morning or evening.
  • On hot days, keep to grass and shade where you can; the AKC warns that asphalt can be hard on paw pads in warm weather.
  • In winter, shorter, more frequent outings suit an older dog better than one long cold one.
  • On icy or very wet days, a slow walk on safe footing near home is enough.

On days when the usual walk is too much

Older dogs have off days. On those, shrink the walk rather than skip outdoor time completely, unless your vet has told you to rest them.

A few things that count as getting out:

  • A slow sniff around the garden or the front of the building.
  • A drive to a quiet green spot, then five minutes of wandering there.
  • Sitting outside together on a step or a bench, letting them watch the street.
  • A scent game indoors: a few pieces of their usual food hidden under a towel.

Our list of fun things to do with your senior dog has more low-energy ideas, and the senior dog bucket list sorts them into good days, slow days and days at home.

When to ask your vet

PDSA advises checking with your vet that the amount of exercise your older dog gets is right for them, and speaking to them if your dog has trouble getting around or seems to have lost interest in exercise. PetMD recommends a vet visit before starting a new exercise routine.

Stop the walk and contact your vet if your dog:

  • limps or seems to be in pain,
  • pants excessively,
  • refuses to carry on.

Chewy's guide also lists groaning, struggling with ordinary things like jumping onto the sofa, shortness of breath and general weakness as reasons to see the vet. Your vet can tell you whether something can be treated and how much walking is safe while it is.

Making the walks you have count

A senior dog's walks are shorter than they were. Our book points out that the ordinary daily walk is still the largest block of time the two of you spend together, and almost nobody has a photo of it.

Two more ideas from our book fit that stage. "Take the Long Way Home" adds a slow extra loop on the way back, not because the dog needs exercise but because there is no reason to hurry. If your dog is tiring, keep the loop small. And "Film an Ordinary Walk" asks for six or seven short clips of the walk you do most days: their back ahead of you, the corner where they always stop, the turn onto your own street. You were going anyway. If you want to keep notes alongside the clips, a senior dog journal is a simple place for them, and our article on how long dogs remember you explains how much of a dog's memory runs on familiar cues like that route.

Questions people ask

How long should I walk my senior dog?

It depends on the dog. PetMD's vet-written guide suggests 30 to 60 minutes of activity a day for many seniors, split into shorter walks, and around 20 minutes a day for dogs with mobility problems. Start from what your dog manages comfortably now and ask your vet what is right for them.

How far should an old dog walk?

Far enough that they come home tired but not sore. Distance matters less than how your dog is afterwards: if they are stiff, lag behind or need a long time to recover, shorten the route. PDSA suggests keeping to a fairly short route in case your dog gets tired.

Should I walk my senior dog every day?

Most older dogs still need the chance to get outdoors every day, even when long walks are no longer possible, according to PDSA. On a stiff day that might be a slow few minutes around the garden or the block.

How do I know if a walk is too much for my old dog?

Signs include stopping or lying down on the walk, lagging behind, heavy panting, and stiffness or trouble moving afterwards. Shorten the next walk. If your dog limps, seems in pain or will not carry on, stop and contact your vet.

What can I do if my old dog can't walk far anymore?

Ask your vet first, so they can check for pain or joint problems. Then keep the outdoor time but shrink it: short sniff walks, a drive to a quiet spot, sitting outside together, and scent games at home.
