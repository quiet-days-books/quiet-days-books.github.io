In short

  • Dogs shouldn't eat candy canes. A regular one is sugar and peppermint, and Pet Poison Helpline says candy isn't a natural part of a dog's diet.
  • Sugar-free candy canes are the emergency: they may contain xylitol, which can drop a dog's blood sugar within 10 to 60 minutes, says the FDA.
  • Xylitol can hide behind other names on the label: the FDA says it is also known as birch sugar or wood sugar.
  • Wrappers and many hard candies can block the throat or gut, sometimes needing surgery, the AKC warns.
  • If your dog ate a candy cane, find the wrapper or box, check for xylitol and chocolate, and call your vet or a pet poison line.

No, dogs shouldn't eat candy canes. A regular candy cane is sugar and peppermint, which can upset a dog's stomach, and the hard pieces and plastic wrapper can cause a blockage. A sugar-free candy cane is more serious: it may contain xylitol, which can drop a dog's blood sugar fast and damage the liver. If your dog ate one, check the wrapper and call your vet.

A dog who crunched one regular candy cane is usually a phone call, not a crisis. A dog who ate a sugar-free one is a drive to the vet. Telling the two apart is the first thing to do, and the wrapper is where you find out.

Why candy canes are bad for dogs

Most candy canes are mostly sugar with peppermint flavoring. Pet Poison Helpline's view is that candy isn't a natural part of a dog's diet and should be avoided as much as possible. Hill's, citing the ASPCA, says candy high in sugar can cause vomiting and diarrhea, and that eating peppermint can cause the same.

Then there's the shape. A candy cane is hard, brittle and usually wrapped in plastic. The AKC says large amounts of hard candy can clump in the stomach and cause an obstruction, and that wrappers can lodge in the throat or intestines and may need surgery to remove.

Sugar-free candy canes and xylitol

This is the version to worry about. Pet Poison Helpline says peppermint candy may contain xylitol, a sugar substitute found in many sugar-free candies and gums. In dogs it can cause a rapid release of insulin, a life-threatening drop in blood sugar and possibly liver failure, and in severe cases it can be fatal.

The FDA explains why dogs react so differently from people: xylitol is absorbed quickly into a dog's blood and triggers a strong insulin release. The drop in blood sugar can happen within 10 to 60 minutes. The FDA also warns that in some cases, low blood sugar and other serious effects may not appear for 12 to 24 hours, which is why a dog may need to stay at the clinic for monitoring even if they look fine at first.

When you read a label, look for more than one word. The FDA says xylitol may also be known as birch sugar or wood sugar. Any candy sold as "sugar-free" or "no sugar added" deserves a close look at the ingredients.

My dog ate a candy cane: what to do

  1. Take away whatever is left, and check that your dog is breathing normally and alert.
  2. Find the wrapper, box or bag. Look for "sugar-free," xylitol, birch sugar or wood sugar, and for chocolate.
  3. Count what's missing, including wrappers and any hooks from the tree.
  4. If xylitol might be involved, call your vet or an emergency clinic right away. The AKC and the FDA both say not to wait.
  5. Out of hours, call ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 (the ASPCA says a consultation fee may apply) or Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661 (its site lists a $99 incident fee).
  6. Don't try to make your dog vomit at home. The AKC says dogs can be made to vomit if it's caught early, within about two hours; that's a decision for your vet or the poison line, and Pet Poison Helpline describes it as something your vet may do at the clinic.

Signs to watch for

For xylitol, the FDA lists vomiting first, followed by signs of low blood sugar: decreased activity, weakness, staggering, poor coordination, collapse and seizures. Pet Poison Helpline adds body tremors and difficulty walking.

For a wrapper or a lump of hard candy stuck in the gut, Pet Poison Helpline lists vomiting, decreased appetite, constipation and lethargy. Its page on dogs and candy also warns that the fat and sugar in candy can lead to pancreatitis. Any of these after a candy cane goes missing is a reason to call your vet.

Candy canes on the tree

Hill's advises against decorating a tree with candy canes in a home with a dog. At nose height, a wrapped cane on a branch is easy to reach, and the dog gets the plastic and sometimes the hook along with the candy. The ASPCA's holiday tips say to keep wrapped food and candy off the floor, and that shards of breakable ornaments can damage a pet's mouth and digestive tract.

If you hang canes anyway, put them high, count them, and use ones you know are not sugar-free. Paper or wooden decorations shaped like canes give the same look without the risk. Our page on dog Christmas photo ideas has more ways to dress the tree around a dog.

What to give your dog instead

If the point is to include your dog, give them something made for dogs or a little plain food they already eat, like a few pieces of plain cooked sweet potato. Keep the candy dish, the stockings and the gift bags of sweets up high, and remember that xylitol turns up in other holiday desserts too, which our page on pumpkin pie and dogs covers.

Questions people ask

Can dogs eat candy canes?

No. A regular candy cane is sugar and peppermint, and sugar can cause vomiting and diarrhea. Sugar-free candy canes may contain xylitol, which is toxic to dogs. The hard pieces and plastic wrapper add a risk of blockage.

My dog ate a candy cane. What should I do?

Take away what's left and find the wrapper or box. If it says sugar-free, xylitol, birch sugar or wood sugar, call your vet or an emergency clinic now. If it was a regular cane, call your vet for advice, especially if the wrapper is missing or your dog is small, older or diabetic.

Is peppermint toxic to dogs?

Peppermint candy isn't the same as the herb. Hill's, citing the ASPCA, says eating peppermint can cause vomiting and diarrhea. The bigger danger in peppermint candy is xylitol in sugar-free versions, says Pet Poison Helpline.

How long after eating xylitol will a dog show signs?

The FDA says a drop in blood sugar can happen within 10 to 60 minutes, and that in some cases serious effects may not show for 12 to 24 hours. A dog who looks fine still needs a vet if xylitol was in the candy.

Can a candy cane wrapper hurt my dog?

Yes. The AKC says wrappers can lodge in the throat or intestinal tract and may need surgery to remove. Pet Poison Helpline lists vomiting, decreased appetite, constipation and lethargy as signs of an obstruction.
