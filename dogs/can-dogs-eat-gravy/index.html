In short

  • The gravy on a holiday table usually isn't safe for dogs: onion or garlic, salty stock cubes, fat from drippings, sometimes wine or cream.
  • The AKC lists gravy among foods not to feed; the ASPCA suggests a drizzle of gravy on a dog's holiday bowl. Both warn about the same ingredients.
  • Stock cubes are the hidden salt: the ASPCA says bouillon used for stock and gravy is high in sodium and can lead to stomach problems and even seizures.
  • If your dog drank gravy or licked the pan, check the recipe and call your vet or a pet poison line.
  • Older dogs and dogs on special diets: ask your vet before any gravy, even a plain one.

Not the gravy on your table. Holiday gravy usually contains onion or garlic, which are toxic to dogs, salt from stock cubes or granules, and fat from the drippings, sometimes with wine or cream on top. The AKC says to keep gravy away from dogs. A small drizzle you know is free of those ingredients is a different case, but ask your vet first, especially with an older dog.

Gravy is the dish where advice online sounds most contradictory. Some pages say never, some say a lick is fine, and even the two biggest US sources word it differently. Once you look at what is in the jug, they turn out to agree.

Is gravy bad for dogs?

Gravy isn't one recipe. It is whatever came out of the roasting pan, thickened, seasoned and often helped along with a stock cube. Whether it is bad for a dog depends on four things that are usually in it.

  • Onion and garlic, cooked with the bird or simmered in the stock.
  • Salt, mostly from stock cubes, granules and seasoning.
  • Fat from the drippings and butter.
  • Wine, sherry or cream in some recipes.

The AKC's Thanksgiving guide lists gravy among the foods not to feed dogs and repeats it in its summary: keep turkey bones, skin, gravy, stuffing and rich or heavily seasoned foods away from dogs.

Why the AKC says no and the ASPCA mentions a drizzle

The ASPCA's Thanksgiving tips suggest giving your dog a small feast of their own, turkey pieces, sweet potato and green beans mixed into their usual dinner, and add "you can even top it off with a drizzle of gravy." The same page warns that onions and garlic are in a lot of Thanksgiving dishes and can be toxic to pets, and that bouillon cubes used for making stocks and gravy contain high amounts of sodium, which can lead to stomach problems and even seizures.

Read together, the two sources describe the same risks. The gravy most families make, with stock cubes, onion and drippings, is exactly the kind both pages warn about. A drizzle only fits the ASPCA's idea if you know what's in it. The ASPCA's general holiday page also recommends keeping your pet's diet the same as any other day, and if you're not sure, that is the simpler choice. If your dog is on a special diet, has a sensitive stomach or is older, ask your vet before you pour anything.

Gravy granules, stock cubes and salt

The ASPCA's people-foods list explains why salt matters: too much can cause increased thirst and urination and, depending on the amount, abnormal electrolytes. Signs of salt toxicity can include vomiting, diarrhea, depression, tremors, seizures and even death.

A spoon of gravy is not the same as the packet. If your dog gets into the stock cubes or the granules jar itself, call your vet. When reading a granules label, look for both salt and onion or garlic in the ingredient list.

Onion and garlic in gravy

Onion, garlic, chives and leeks belong to the Allium family. The ASPCA says they can cause stomach irritation and red blood cell damage that can lead to anemia, depending on the amount eaten. Pet Poison Helpline adds that some breeds are more sensitive, naming Japanese breeds such as the Akita and the Shiba Inu.

In gravy the onion often isn't visible. It was roasted under the turkey or simmered in the stock. Unless you made the gravy yourself and left them out, assume they're in it.

My dog ate gravy: what do I do?

A lick from a plate is a different event from a dog who drank the gravy boat or licked the roasting tin clean. Either way, the ingredients matter more than the mess.

  1. Take the rest away and check that your dog is breathing and acting normally.
  2. Check what went into the gravy: onion, garlic, stock cubes or granules, wine.
  3. Estimate how much is gone. The ASPCA asks owners to note the amount eaten.
  4. Call your vet. Out of hours, ASPCA Animal Poison Control is at (888) 426-4435 (the ASPCA says a consultation fee may apply) and Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661 (its site lists a $99 per-incident fee, follow-up calls included).
  5. Don't make your dog vomit unless the vet or poison line says to. Pet Poison Helpline says not to induce vomiting without consulting a vet or the helpline.
  6. If the gravy had wine in it, mention it. The ASPCA says alcohol is absorbed quickly and asks for prompt veterinary attention.

Fat, drippings and an older dog's stomach

Drippings are the base of most gravy, and they carry the fat from the roast. The AKC warns that turkey skin is high in fat and often coated with butter or oil, which raises the risk of pancreatitis and other digestive problems. The ASPCA's holiday page puts it more broadly: fatty, sweet and spicy foods can all be dangerous to pets.

An older dog may be on a diet chosen by your vet for a reason, and a holiday is when that diet is most likely to slip. If yours is, the vet's plan wins over any list on the internet, this one included.

A holiday bowl without the gravy

You can give your dog the smell of the day without the seasoning. Set aside some plain turkey before the bird is carved for the table: boneless, fully cooked and without skin, as the AKC and ASPCA both advise. Mix a little into their usual food with plain green beans or sweet potato.

Then watch them eat it. Our book One Day, You'll Miss This has a page called "Capture Meal Time": film the whole thing, from the moment the cupboard opens to the last inspection of an empty bowl. A holiday dinner is the version of that ritual with the most waiting in it.

Questions people ask

Can dogs eat gravy?

Not the gravy made for people. It usually contains onion or garlic, which are toxic to dogs, salty stock cubes and fat. The AKC says to keep gravy away from dogs. The ASPCA mentions a drizzle of gravy on a dog's holiday meal, so a small amount you know has none of those ingredients is a different case; ask your vet first.

Can dogs have gravy made from granules or stock cubes?

It's not a good idea. The ASPCA says bouillon cubes used for stock and gravy contain high amounts of sodium, which can lead to stomach problems and even seizures. Read the label of any granules for salt, onion and garlic.

My dog licked the gravy boat. Is that dangerous?

How serious a lick is depends on what was in the gravy and how much was left. Check for onion, garlic, wine and stock cubes, and call your vet or a pet poison line if you're unsure.

Is turkey drippings gravy OK for dogs?

Drippings carry the fat from the roast. The AKC warns that fatty turkey skin raises the risk of pancreatitis and digestive upset, and pan juices usually carry the onion, garlic and salt from the roast. Skip it.

What can I give my dog instead of gravy?

A little plain, boneless, cooked turkey mixed into their usual food gives the holiday smell without the seasoning. The ASPCA also suggests sweet potato and green beans. Ask your vet if your dog is on a special diet.
