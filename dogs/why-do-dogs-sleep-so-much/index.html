In short

  • Comparative sleep databases put the average dog at about 10.1 hours of sleep a day; studies report anything from 7.7 to 16 hours.
  • Dogs sleep mostly at night but rest in the afternoon too, in short cycles, so they look asleep for much of the day.
  • A busier day means more sleep: in an EEG study, dogs slept more after an active afternoon.
  • Older dogs nap more by day and may wake more at night; a big change in that pattern is worth noting and showing your vet.
  • Sleep is easy to record without waking a dog: a minute of video, two minutes of breathing, a photo of the spot.

Dogs sleep about 10 hours a day on average, and much of the rest of the time they lie quietly between naps, so they seem to sleep most of the day. They sleep mainly at night, rest again in the afternoon, and sleep more after a busy day. Older dogs shift more of their sleep into daytime naps.

How many hours a day do dogs sleep?

There is no single number, but there is a usual one. A 2020 review of dog sleep research by Bódizs and colleagues notes that comparative sleep databases use 10.1 hours a day for the domestic dog, against 7 to 8 hours for people. Individual studies report anything from 7.7 to 16 hours, partly because researchers disagree on whether to count drowsiness, a light, dozy state that takes up about 21% of a dog's day, as sleep.

The puppy figures come from owners' estimates, not from monitors, so read them as a rough guide. The same paper sums up earlier work: adult dogs sleep between 60% and 80% of the night (8pm to 8am) and between 3% and 28% of the day.

Why do dogs sleep so much?

Part of the answer is that "so much" is measured against us. A dog sleeping 10 hours and dozing for a few more looks idle next to a person awake for 16 hours. The other part is how dog sleep is built.

Dogs sleep in short cycles. The review describes cycles of about 20 minutes, around 12 minutes of drowsiness or non-REM sleep and 6 minutes of REM, compared with roughly 90-minute cycles in humans. So a long stretch on the sofa can be many short sleeps, with dozing in between.

How the day went matters too. In a 2018 study, researchers recorded the brain activity of 16 family dogs as they slept. After an active day, the dogs slept more, fell into drowsiness and deeper sleep sooner, and spent more time in non-REM and REM sleep.

Where a dog sleeps changes how well it sleeps. Earlier research summarised in the review found that dogs sleeping indoors spent 80% of the night asleep, dogs in a yard 70%, and dogs outdoors in an unfenced area 60%.

Why do dogs sleep so much during the day?

Because their day has a built-in quiet stretch. The review notes that most of a dog's sleep falls between about 9pm and 6am, with a period of rest in the afternoon, and that night sleep is more efficient and less broken than afternoon naps.

A 2020 study at North Carolina State University put activity monitors on 42 healthy pet dogs aged two to nine for 14 days. On average, activity peaked at about 7am and 7pm and was lowest around noon. The dogs were more active at weekends than on weekdays, which suggests their days follow the household's.

That fits a wider idea in the research. Most wild canines, such as foxes and wolves, are active at night, at dawn and dusk, or around the clock, while pet dogs are active by day. The review's authors suggest this is an adaptation to living with people. So a dog in a quiet house on a weekday, with everyone out, has every reason to sleep.

Why do older dogs sleep so much?

Sleep changes shape with age. The review cites research showing that ageing dogs have less REM sleep, more non-REM sleep during the day and more wakefulness at night: shorter, more broken sleep on both sides of the clock. In the NC State study, younger dogs were significantly more active than older ones during the morning and evening peaks. Kinsman's paper also sums up earlier work showing older dogs sleep substantially more, with shorter but more frequent naps during the day.

Most of this is ordinary ageing. An older body tires sooner, and naps fill the gaps. If you are not sure whether your dog counts as old yet, our senior dog age chart by breed shows where the line falls, and our guide to how much to walk a senior dog helps keep the active hours good ones.

Why is my dog sleeping so much all of a sudden?

A sudden change is different from a slow one. Cornell University's vet college lists sleep-wake changes among the signs of dementia in senior dogs and notes they are often the first sign families notice: a dog that sleeps the day away while you are at work and then walks the house at night. Cornell suggests writing down what you see and taking the notes to your vet.

We are not vets, so here is only what to note and pass on:

  1. How many hours your dog seems to sleep now, compared with a month ago.
  2. Whether your dog is hard to wake, or sleeps through things that used to get them up, like meals or the door.
  3. Any restlessness, pacing or waking you at night.
  4. Anything else new: eating, drinking, stiffness, interest in walks.
  5. A short video of the sleep or the night-time behaviour.

Then ask your vet. Many changes are just age, and the vet is the one to tell.

Why do dogs twitch so much in their sleep?

Twitches belong to REM sleep. The review describes REM in dogs as a state of reduced muscle tone with occasional bursts of activity, such as twitches, along with rapid eye movements and quick, irregular breathing. Paws paddling or lips catching on a tooth during a nap are normal REM sleep.

That is also why it is worth watching rather than waking: these few minutes are the most expressive part of a dog's sleep.

Keeping a record of how your dog sleeps

A dog spends a large share of its life asleep in front of you, and almost none of it ends up in a photo. Our book One Day, You'll Miss This has three pages for this. "Film Them Sleeping" asks for one minute of video of your dog asleep, exactly where they actually sleep, without moving the blanket for a better shot. "Record Their Breathing While They Sleep" asks for two minutes of audio, phone on the floor, television off. "Photograph Their Favorite Sleeping Spot" asks for two photos of the spot, one with your dog in it and one empty, with the shape still in the bed.

Those recordings help in another way too: if your dog's sleep ever changes, you will have something to compare it with. For more ideas in the same spirit, see our senior dog photoshoot ideas and the senior dog bucket list, which sorts ideas by how much energy your dog has left.

Questions people ask

How many hours a day does a dog sleep?

About 10 hours on average. A 2020 review of dog sleep research notes that comparative databases use 10.1 hours a day for dogs, while individual studies report between 7.7 and 16 hours. Owners of 12-month-old dogs in a UK study reported 10.8 hours on average.

Why do dogs sleep so much during the day?

Dogs sleep mostly between about 9pm and 6am but also rest in the afternoon. In a study of 42 pet dogs wearing activity monitors, activity peaked near 7am and 7pm and dipped around noon. A dog home alone, or with nothing going on, fills those quiet hours with naps.

Why do older dogs sleep so much?

Sleep shifts with age. Research reviewed in 2020 found ageing dogs had less REM sleep, more deep sleep during the day and more wakefulness at night. Cornell's vet college notes that changes in the sleep-wake cycle are often the first sign families notice of age-related cognitive changes.

Should I worry if my dog sleeps all day?

Not if it fits your dog's age, the day it has had and its usual pattern. A dog that sleeps much more than usual, is hard to wake, or starts pacing at night while sleeping through the day has changed, and that is worth showing your vet.

Do puppies sleep more than adult dogs?

Yes, a little. In a UK study of more than 2,000 puppies, owners reported 11.2 hours of sleep a day on average at 16 weeks and 10.8 hours at 12 months. The younger dogs slept more in the daytime and slightly less at night.
