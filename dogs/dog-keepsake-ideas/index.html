The best dog keepsake ideas are things you make now, with your dog asleep on the floor beside you: a dated paw print, a pinch of fur from the brush, the old collar and tag, the toy they have chewed past recognition, and a few recordings of the sounds they make. Each one takes minutes. Write the date on all of them.

Search for dog keepsakes and most of what comes up is made after a dog has gone. This page is about the other kind, the ones you make while the dog is still here to lean on you during the process.

None of these needs a shop. Some people later turn a print or a photo into jewellery or a framed piece, and that can be lovely, but the raw material comes first and only you can collect it.

Dog paw print keepsake ideas

A paw print surprises almost everyone with its size. Pressed flat at actual scale, it is bigger or smaller than you expected, and a photo never shows that.

Our book One Day, You'll Miss This has a page for this, "Make a Paw Print". The method is simple:

  1. Use a paw-print kit made for pets, such as ink-free paper or a soft clay tray, or another non-toxic, washable product sold for the purpose. Follow its instructions.
  2. Pick a time when your dog is relaxed, ideally half asleep on the floor.
  3. Press the paw gently and briefly, then let go. If they pull away, stop and try another day.
  4. On the back, or pressed into the clay, write the date, their weight, their height at the shoulder and their length from nose to tail.
  5. If it works, do a second one the same day, so one can go in a frame and one in a drawer.
  6. Photograph the print as well, in case the paper fades or tears.

Step 4 is the part people skip, and it turns a shape into a record of one dog at one age.

A word on salt dough, which turns up in a lot of DIY paw print ideas. Pet Poison Helpline warns that as few as one or two salt dough ornaments could make a dog very sick from salt poisoning, and that the baked dough can be hard to pass. The ASPCA lists tremors, seizures and even death among the signs of salt toxicity in pets. A pet kit avoids the problem. If you do use salt dough, keep the dough and the finished print well out of reach, and call your vet if your dog eats any.

A nose print is the odd cousin of the paw print. The book's "Make a Nose Print" page asks for a pet-safe product designed for noses, kept well clear of the nostrils and eyes, one attempt and no holding the head. Plenty of dogs refuse. A close photograph of the nose in daylight is a complete record on its own.

Dog hair keepsake ideas

You are already collecting dog hair without trying. It is on the sofa and in the car. Keeping a small amount on purpose is different, and it takes ten seconds at the end of something you were doing anyway.

Next time you brush them, keep what comes out of the brush. A pinch, not a handful. Put it in a tiny glass jar, an envelope or a locket, and write the date on it. That is the whole of "Save a Tiny Lock of Fur" in our book, plus one more step: do it again in a few years. Coats change with age, in colour and thickness, and two dated samples side by side show something one never will.

The rule that matters: only naturally shed fur from the brush or from grooming. Never cut it from your dog for a keepsake.

If you want to turn the fur into something else later, a felted shape or a keyring, a jar of dated fur gives you plenty to work with. Store it dry and away from sunlight.

Dog collar keepsake ideas

A collar is easy to overlook because it is always there. The tag has an old phone number on it, or the address of a flat you moved out of. The worn patch where the buckle sits is a record of every walk.

"Photograph Their Collar and Tag" in our book asks you to take it off for five minutes, indoors with the doors shut, and photograph it laid flat on a plain surface in daylight: the whole collar, the tag close enough to read every word, and the back, where the wear is. If you have kept earlier collars, photograph them in order. Lined up, they show every size your dog has been.

Two practical notes. The photo of the tag carries your phone number and address, so cover those before you share it anywhere. And if you want to keep the smell, the book suggests a bandana they slept on for a week, sealed in a bag with the date on it. It is optional, and the smell will not last long.

When a collar is eventually replaced, the old one goes in the box. That is the natural moment, so you never need to take a working collar off your dog for good.

The toy that has seen better days

Every dog seems to have one toy that is no longer recognisable as what it was. Our book's "Preserve Their Favorite Toy" page says to photograph it as it is now, without cleaning it up: the missing eye, the repaired seam, the ear that came off one winter. Then photograph your dog holding it, because that is the only picture that explains why anyone would keep a ruined piece of fabric.

When it gets to loose pieces, exposed stuffing or a squeaker working its way out, take it out of use. It does not have to leave the house. It goes in the keepsake box and stops being something they carry around.

Keepsakes you can hear

Sounds are the hardest thing to describe later, even to yourself: claws on the kitchen floor, the sigh when they lie down, the noise at the door. Recording them costs nothing and fits on a phone.

  • Their footsteps across a hard floor, thirty seconds, phone held low, without calling them.
  • Their breathing while they sleep, phone on the floor beside them, television off.
  • The greeting when you come home, filmed from your side as the door opens.

The book's "Build Their Sound Library" page asks you to gather these into one folder, named the same way, then listen to all of it in order and notice which sound you know well and have not caught yet. Our senior dog bucket list has more recording ideas, sorted by how much energy your dog has.

How to put together a dog keepsake memory box

A keepsake memory box does not need to be bought. A shoebox, a wooden box from a charity shop or a large envelope works. What makes it useful is that everything inside is dated and labelled.

What to put in it:

  • The paw print, with weight and size on the back.
  • An envelope or jar of brushed fur, dated.
  • The old collar and tag, once replaced.
  • The retired favourite toy.
  • Two or three printed photos, including one of the collar and one of the paw.
  • A card with every nickname they have had, and a line about what they do each morning.

Keep digital copies of the photos and sound files in one folder too, with a backup. Paper fades and boxes get damp; files can be copied.

If you would rather keep things on pages than in a box, our list of dog memory book ideas covers what to write and which photos to choose, and a senior dog journal is a lighter version you add to as you go.

Keepsakes as gifts for someone with an old dog

If you are looking for dog keepsake gifts for a friend, the most useful thing is often time and help rather than an object: offer to hold the phone while they film their dog, or to do the paw print together, one person steadying and one pressing. A blank keepsake box with a few envelopes and labels inside is a practical present too, since it gives them somewhere to put what they collect.

Whatever you choose, the keepsakes themselves come from the dog and the person who lives with them. They are best made on an ordinary day, while the dog is snoring through it.

Questions people ask

What are good dog keepsake ideas?

Things you can make or keep while your dog is alive: a paw print on paper or clay with the date and their weight written beside it, a nose print if they tolerate it, a pinch of fur saved after brushing, the old collar and tag when you replace them, their most destroyed toy, and short recordings of their footsteps or greeting.

How do I make a paw print of my dog at home?

Use a paw-print kit sold for pets, such as ink-free paper or a soft clay tray, and follow its instructions. Do it when your dog is relaxed or half asleep, press the paw gently and briefly, and stop if they pull away. Write the date, their weight and height on the back.

Can I use salt dough for a dog paw print?

It is safer not to. Salt dough is very salty, and Pet Poison Helpline says as few as one or two salt dough ornaments could make a dog very sick. If you do make one, keep the dough and the finished print well out of reach. If your dog eats some, call your vet.

What can I do with my dog's old collar?

Photograph it laid flat in daylight, the whole collar and then the tag close up, plus the worn back. Then keep it in a box or envelope with the date. Lined up with earlier collars, it shows every size your dog has been.

What goes in a dog keepsake memory box?

Dated items: a paw print, an envelope of brushed fur, an old collar and tag, a retired favourite toy, a printed photo or two, and a note with their nicknames and a few things they do every day. Keep a copy of photos and sound files somewhere safe too.
