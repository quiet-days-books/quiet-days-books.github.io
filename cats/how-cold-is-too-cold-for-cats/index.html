The question splits in two: a cat who goes out in winter, and a cat at home in a house that gets chilly at night. The numbers and the advice differ a little, so we take them one at a time.

Do cats get cold?

Yes. Cats Protection says that despite their coats, cats feel the cold like humans and other animals. Their normal body temperature is 38 to 39°C (100 to 102°F), only slightly higher than ours, and they need to keep it there. They are also descended from the African wildcat, a desert animal, which is why they lean toward warmth.

The same charity notes that cats are usually very good at finding the cosiest place in the house, and that their fur insulates well. So on a day that feels chilly to you, your cat may be perfectly comfortable on the bed. Some breeds, such as Siberian cats, have adapted to colder climates.

How cold is too cold for cats outside?

Texas A&M's vet school says fur thickness, coat length and body mass all matter, which makes it hard to name one exact dangerous temperature. These are the figures we found stated on the pages themselves:

Cats Protection adds that how risky the cold is depends on the cat and the conditions. Elderly cats, unwell cats and cats with little or no hair are at higher risk, and the charity suggests keeping them indoors when it is cold. Damp makes it worse: a wet coat feels colder, so Cats Protection suggests drying a cat with a towel when they come in, if they are happy to be handled.

How cold is too cold for an indoor cat?

This is the question for the cat who never goes out, in a flat where you turn the heating down to save money or keep it off while you are at work.

Cats Protection's answer is direct: unless the temperature indoors drops to seven degrees Celsius (45°F) or lower, there is no need to put the heating on for your cat's benefit. Even when you feel chilly, their fur is helping to keep them warm.

What helps more than heat is a choice of places. Cats Protection suggests cardboard boxes lined with blankets or old jumpers, in quiet, dry spots away from draughts, which your cat can reach whenever they like. PDSA suggests raising beds off the floor so they don't sit in a cold draught, and the ASPCA says the same: a warm place to sleep, off the floor and away from drafts. Cats Protection also mentions heat pads and beds that hang over radiators.

How to tell if your cat is cold

Cats Protection says a chilly cat will most likely seek out somewhere cosy and curl up into a ball, and if they are otherwise behaving normally, there is no need to worry. The charity's list of signs that a cat is too cold:

  • shivering
  • curling tightly into a ball
  • being more tired than usual, or lethargic
  • cold ears, paws or tail

Cats Protection also notes that cats may sleep more in winter to conserve energy and hold on to body heat, and that they look for sunny spots while daylight is short. Our page on why cats sleep so much covers the normal amount.

If your cat is cold to the touch and doesn't warm up indoors, seems weak, or moves slowly or clumsily, call your vet straight away. Don't try to warm them fast with a hot water bottle or heat pad; Cats Protection warns against burning them.

Where your cat goes when the house cools

You can learn a lot about your cat's comfort by watching where they settle across a winter day. Cats Protection notes that cats look for sunny spots and cosy places in winter, so the spot may change with the light: a patch of sun on the floor, the radiator shelf, your side of the bed after you get up.

Three activities in our book The House Your Cat Knows fit this well:

  1. "The First Place in the Morning": the first time you notice your cat today, write down where they are. On a cold morning that is usually the warmest spot in the house.
  2. "The Nap Rotation": over a day or a week, note two or three places your cat rests, and what the light, heat or noise is like in each.
  3. "The Window They Choose": describe the window your cat returns to and what the light is doing at the time of day it matters most. The book's own rule is to use a closed, secured window and never open one for the page, which suits winter well.

Done for a week, these give you a map of your cat's warm places. If a favourite spot sits in a draught, you'll see it, and you can put a lined box or a raised bed where they already like to be. The book asks you never to wake or move a sleeping cat for a note or photo, and the same goes here.

Keeping a cat who goes outside safe in winter

If your cat goes out, the advice from Cats Protection, PDSA and Texas A&M comes down to a short list:

  • Make sure they can always get back in, ideally through a cat flap, and check the flap after snow or ice in case it is frozen or blocked (Cats Protection, PDSA).
  • Bring them in at night, with a litter tray indoors so they don't have to go out (Cats Protection).
  • Keep fresh water inside in case outdoor water freezes (Cats Protection).
  • Check sheds, garages and outbuildings before you shut them, so no cat gets locked in (Cats Protection).
  • Wipe road grit, salt and anything else off their paws and fur when they come in (Cats Protection).
  • Keep their microchip details up to date in case they wander looking for warmth (Cats Protection).
  • Don't leave a cat in a parked car in cold weather: the ASPCA says cars can hold in the cold like a refrigerator.

Check the car engine and clean up antifreeze

The PetMD and Chewy pages we opened don't mention these two hazards. Cats Protection, PDSA and Texas A&M all warn about both.

Cats looking for warmth may crawl under a car or up into the engine. PDSA says cats often shelter under cars in cold and wet weather or climb inside the bonnet for the warm engine. Before you start the car, knock on the bonnet (the hood, in American English), and PDSA also suggests checking under the bonnet. Texas A&M gives the same advice: thump the hood with your hand first.

Antifreeze is the other one. Cats Protection says antifreeze, screen wash and de-icer can contain ethylene glycol, that it only takes a small amount to harm a cat, and that even a few licks can cause serious harm such as kidney failure. A cat can swallow it just by walking through a puddle and grooming. Texas A&M notes that pets like the taste. What to do:

  • Choose screen wash and de-icer without ethylene glycol; the ASPCA suggests products with propylene glycol instead.
  • Wipe up any spill straight away and keep containers out of reach.

PDSA says that if you think your pet has licked antifreeze, get them to the vet at once and never wait for signs to appear. In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control line is (888) 426-4435.

Questions people ask

Do cats get cold?

Yes. Cats Protection says cats feel the cold like humans and other animals despite their coats. They normally keep a body temperature of 38 to 39°C, and they descend from the African wildcat, so they prefer warmth.

What temperature is too cold for a cat outside?

Cats Protection says anything below about 7°C (45°F) can start to be unsafe for a cat to stay outside, especially for long periods. Texas A&M says indoor pets not used to cold should not be left out when the average daily temperature is below 45°F.

Is my house too cold for my cat?

Cats Protection says there is no need to put the heating on for your cat unless the temperature indoors drops to 7°C (45°F) or lower. Give them draught-free beds off the floor and they will usually find the warmest spot.

How do I know if my cat is cold?

Cats Protection lists shivering, curling tightly into a ball, being more tired than usual, and cold ears, paws or tail. If your cat stays cold, weak or unusually slow after warming up indoors, call your vet.

Should I keep my cat in at night in winter?

Cats Protection advises making sure your cat comes in at night, with a litter tray indoors, and bringing them in during extreme weather such as blizzards or storms.
