In short

  • Chattering is a fast, mostly silent clashing of the jaws, usually at a bird or other prey the cat can see but not reach.
  • Nobody knows for certain why cats do it. Excitement and frustration are the explanations Cats Protection lists first.
  • The 'copying prey' theory rests on one field report of a wild margay imitating monkey calls, not on studies of pet cats.
  • Chattering is different from the chirp, a short bird-like call, and from the trill, a friendly greeting.
  • It's normal. If your cat seems frustrated at the window, give it a hunt it can finish with a wand toy.

Cats chatter when they see prey they can't get to, most often a bird or a squirrel on the other side of a window. The jaw trembles fast, sometimes with small clicks. Nobody knows for certain why. Cats Protection lists excitement and frustration first, and the idea that cats copy their prey rests on very little evidence.

Below is what chattering is, each theory and how well it holds up, and what to do if your cat chatters a lot.

What is cat chattering?

Cats Protection describes it as a cat quickly opening and closing its mouth, also called twittering or chirping. It usually happens indoors, when the cat can see birds, rodents or other prey through a window or door.

A 2020 review of cat sounds in the Journal of Veterinary Science gives a closer description. In chattering, the cat rhythmically clashes its jaws, making a quiet, smacking sound. It is mostly voiceless, which means the vocal cords barely vibrate, and it is made near prey that is out of reach. The review notes that a run of chattering can mix in other sounds, so a single outburst may also contain chirps.

You will usually see the rest of the hunt in the body too: eyes fixed on the target, ears forward, the tail moving from side to side, sometimes a crouch. Our guide to cat tail behavior meaning helps read that last part.

The review itself is blunt about it: it is not sure why cats chatter.

Excitement and frustration: the leading idea

Cats Protection lists this first. It says chattering could be a display of excitement, or the result of a surge of adrenaline at prey the cat wants and can't reach.

It fits when chattering happens. Cats Protection says it is more common in indoor cats who don't have access to wildlife outdoors. The AAFP and ISFM guidelines on a cat's environmental needs explain why the urge is so strong: predatory behaviour happens even in well-fed cats, and a cat that can hunt spends a large part of its day doing it. A bird behind glass sets the whole sequence going, and the glass stops it at the first step.

Some behaviourists, quoted by Chewy, read the same scene more cheerfully, as enthusiasm and entertainment rather than frustration. Both may be true for different cats, which is why the rest of the body matters.

Do cats chatter to imitate birds?

This is the theory that sounds the most impressive, and the evidence is thin.

It goes back largely to one observation. On 12 October 2005, researchers studying pied tamarins, small monkeys, in a forest reserve near Manaus, Brazil, heard calls like those of tamarin young coming from the trees. A tamarin on lookout went to investigate and warned the group. About twenty minutes later a margay, a small wild cat, appeared and moved toward the feeding monkeys, which fled. Fabiano Calleia and colleagues published it in 2009 as the first field record of a margay imitating its prey. Local people had told them that pumas, jaguars and ocelots also copy the calls of animals such as agoutis.

That is interesting, but it shows a wild cat making prey-like calls, not a house cat chattering. The 2020 review does mention the idea that some cat sounds copy prey, and says the chirp may have been named for sounding like a bird or rodent. No study we found has shown that a pet cat's chattering draws birds closer.

Chattering, chirping or trilling?

People often use these words for the same thing, but they are different sounds.

  • Chattering: a fast clash of the jaws, little or no voice, aimed at prey out of reach.
  • Chirp: a short, high-pitched call that the 2020 review compares to a bird's chirp, used when the cat wants something. A run of them is called chirrups.
  • Trill: International Cat Care calls the trill or chirrup a friendly greeting, one of the murmuring sounds cats tend to make with the mouth closed, like the purr, and sometimes aimed at people.

So a cat chattering at the window is watching a bird. A cat trilling as you come through the door is saying hello. If your cat brings you its "catch" afterwards, our article on why your cat brings you toys picks up that side of the hunting story.

Is chattering bad for my cat?

No, it is a normal part of being a cat. Not every cat does it, and a cat that never chatters is not missing anything.

Cats Protection makes one exception: if your cat starts chattering a lot more than usual, it is best to have it checked by a vet.

What to do if your cat chatters a lot

First decide which cat you have. A cat that chatters, watches for a while and then wanders off to nap is enjoying the view. A cat that paces between windows, lashes its tail or seems wound up afterwards is more likely frustrated.

For a frustrated cat, Cats Protection and the AAFP and ISFM guidelines suggest:

  1. Give it a hunt it can finish. Play with a wand toy every day, in short sessions, moving it like a bird swooping or a mouse running away.
  2. Let the cat catch the toy at the end. That is the step the window never allows.
  3. Offer a treat after play, as the environmental needs guidelines suggest.
  4. Add food it has to work for, such as a puzzle feeder or a feeding ball.
  5. If one window winds it up, cover its lower part with card or a window sticker, as Cats Protection advises.

For a contented watcher, the advice runs the other way. Chewy's experts suggest a window perch, a cat tree near the glass, even a bird feeder outside. Just watch how your cat takes it the first few days.

The window your cat chooses

Most chattering happens at one particular window. It is rarely the nicest one in the house; it is the one with the bird table, the hedge, the gutter where pigeons land.

Our book The House Your Cat Knows has an activity called The Window They Choose. It asks you to photograph or describe the window your cat already uses, note what the light is doing, and the time of day the spot seems to matter most. The book is firm that it should be a closed, secured window, never one opened for the page. Another activity, The Game They Already Know, asks you to describe a play pattern your cat already has and to stop while the game still feels easy.

Questions people ask

Why do cats chatter?

Usually because they can see prey they can't reach, such as a bird outside a window. Cats Protection says nobody knows for certain why, but excitement, or a surge of adrenaline at prey they want and can't get, are the leading ideas. A 2020 review of cat sounds lists it as displacement, a response to wanting prey that is out of reach.

Is it normal for cats to chatter?

Yes. Cats Protection calls it a natural behaviour, more often seen in indoor cats who can't get to wildlife outdoors. Not every cat does it. If your cat starts chattering far more than it used to, Cats Protection suggests a vet check.

Are cats trying to imitate birds when they chatter?

That is one theory. The evidence behind it is thin: one field report from Brazil of a wild margay making calls like young tamarins, and local accounts of other wild cats copying prey. No study has shown that pet cats chatter to lure birds.

What is the difference between chattering and chirping?

Chattering is a rhythmic clash of the jaws, with little or no voice. A chirp is a short, high-pitched call, and a trill is a soft, rolling sound cats use as a friendly greeting, sometimes to people. A cat at the window may mix chatters and chirps in one burst.

Should I stop my cat from chattering?

No need to stop the sound itself. Watch the rest of the cat. If it looks relaxed and goes back to napping, the window is entertainment. If it paces, lashes its tail or gets tense, give it short daily play that ends in a catch, and consider covering the lower part of that window, as Cats Protection suggests.
