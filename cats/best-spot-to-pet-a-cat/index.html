In short

  • Most friendly cats prefer touch on the cheeks, the base of the ears and under the chin: the places they rub on objects anyway.
  • Avoid the belly and the base of the tail. Go carefully on the back, flanks, legs and tail.
  • Let the cat start. Stroke for 3-5 seconds, pause, and see if it pushes back for more.
  • A cat turning its head, flattening its ears, twitching its tail or rippling its skin is asking for a break.
  • Some cats would rather sit near you than be touched. Sitting near you is how they choose your company.

The best spot to pet a cat is the face: the cheeks, the base of the ears and, for many cats, under the chin. Avoid the belly and the base of the tail, and go carefully on the back, flanks and legs. Let your cat start the contact, stroke for a few seconds, then pause and see whether it asks for more.

That is the short map. The longer answer is that every cat redraws it, and the cat in your house has its own version you can learn.

Where do cats like to be pet? The map most cats share

International Cat Care uses a traffic-light picture, based on the work of Dr Lauren Finka, to show where most cats prefer and dislike being touched. The table follows it and the CAT guidelines tested at Battersea (more on those below):

Zone Where Why
Green Cheeks, around the chin, base of the ears Scent glands sit here, and cats rub these areas on objects all the time, so touch there feels familiar
Amber Back, flanks, legs, tail Cats rub these less on things, so touch can feel more like a threat. Go carefully, especially with a cat you don't know well
Red Belly, base of the tail, paws Sensitive areas, easily hurt in a fight. Paws are also how a cat escapes, so touching them may get you scratched

The research behind it points the same way. A 2021 paper on cats at Battersea Dogs and Cats Home, summing up earlier studies, says touch on the sides of the head (the temporal region, between eye and ear) tends to bring the most positive responses, and touch near the tail the opposite. Reactions to the mouth area, flanks, stomach and back vary more from cat to cat.

Why is the face the best spot to pet a cat?

Watch a relaxed cat walk past a chair leg or a door frame. It leads with the cheek, then often the side of the body. International Cat Care describes cats rubbing against each other in the same way, mostly around the mouth and cheeks, as one of their main ways of communicating by touch, with scent swapped at the same time.

When your hand goes to your cat's cheek, you are joining something it already does. When your cat pushes its face into your knuckles, it is choosing the spot for you. That push is the best guide you have.

Where not to pet a cat: belly, tail base and long strokes

Start with the belly. International Cat Care points out that a cat rolling onto its back in front of you is usually a friendly greeting and a sign it feels relaxed with you. It is not an invitation for a belly rub.

The base of the tail is the second. In a 2015 study by Sarah Ellis and colleagues, cats were stroked on different parts of the body. The tail-end region drew the highest negative scores, both from a stranger and from a familiar person. A few cats seem to like a scratch there, and if yours is one you already know. For any other cat, leave it.

Then there are long head-to-tail strokes. They run the whole length of the back and take your hand from the green zone to the red one. If you like stroking that way, stop at the shoulders and watch how the cat responds before going further.

What is the best way to pet a cat? Choice, attention, touch

In 2021, Camilla Haywood, Lauren Finka and colleagues tested a simple set of guidelines called CAT with 100 cats waiting for new homes at Battersea and 120 members of the public. After a short training session, people's handling matched the guidelines more closely, and the cats showed more friendly behaviour, such as rubbing, kneading and tail waving, and less aggression towards people.

Here is the method, in the order the study used it:

  1. Sit or crouch and offer your hand. Do not reach over the cat or approach from behind. International Cat Care suggests approaching from the side and getting down to the cat's level.
  2. Let the cat come to you. If it rubs against your hand, it wants contact. If it doesn't, don't stroke it.
  3. Stay with the face. Base of the ears, cheeks, and under the chin if your cat likes it.
  4. Stroke for three to five seconds, then pause. Both the study and International Cat Care use this check. When you stop, does your cat push back into your hand?
  5. Push back means carry on. No push, a turned head or a step away means it is time for a break.
  6. Let it leave. Do not follow a cat that walks off to keep the petting going.

The study's authors noted that the guidelines are more hands-off than most people would choose.

How do you know your cat has had enough?

These signs come from the CAT guidelines and from International Cat Care. Any one of them means pause:

  • turning the head away or moving off
  • ears flattening or rotating backwards
  • shaking the head
  • fur rippling along the back
  • licking the nose or lips
  • going still and stopping purring or rubbing
  • turning sharply to look at your hand
  • a sudden short burst of self-grooming
  • the tail twitching or swishing, especially held low

Purring on its own does not prove a cat is enjoying itself. Read it with the rest of the body. If you would like to capture a contented purr, our guide on how to record cat purring does it without asking anything of the cat.

Does it matter who is petting the cat?

It might, and not in the way you would expect. In the 2015 Ellis study, cats showed more negative reactions to being stroked by their familiar person than by an unfamiliar one. The authors themselves called for more research, and it was a small study. Our own reading, and it is only a reading: owners may fall into stroking in ways their cat puts up with rather than enjoys.

The 2021 paper adds a caution in the same direction: a cat that does not lash out is not necessarily enjoying the contact. Tolerating and wanting are different things.

Why does my cat follow me but not want to be pet?

People type this question often. International Cat Care says not all cats want regular physical contact, and some prefer to sit near you, or on your warm lap, without being stroked. Play with a wand toy is another way to be together without touch.

A cat that follows you from room to room and settles one cushion away has made its choice of company. Our page on why your cat follows you looks at the reasons. Our book The House Your Cat Knows has a page for exactly this, "Near, Not Touching", for cats who keep company and keep all touch on their own terms. Its rule is short: do not invite touch to improve the page.

How to find your own cat's favourite spot to be pet

The map above is an average. Your cat's own map takes a week or two of paying attention:

  • Hold still and let the cat steer. Rest your hand where your cat can reach it. Where does it push: cheek, top of the head, chin, the side of the neck?
  • Notice what it rubs on. Door frames, table legs and the corners of books show the angles your cat likes.
  • Change one thing at a time. Same spot, lighter touch. Same touch, shorter time. See which version gets a push back.
  • Note the time and place. Your cat may be more open to contact in some places and moods than others: on the bed at night, but not by the food bowl.
  • Write it down. One line is enough: "Chin yes, top of head no, sofa evenings best." Our cat daily routine page suggests a simple format.

International Cat Care notes that preferences can change as a cat gets older, and that a sudden change in how a cat responds to touch is worth raising with your vet.

Questions people ask

Where do cats like to be pet the most?

Around the cheeks, at the base of the ears and, for many cats, under the chin. International Cat Care explains that cats have scent glands there and rub those areas on objects often, so touch there feels familiar rather than threatening.

Where should you not pet a cat?

The belly and the base of the tail. Research on stroking found the tail end drew the most negative reactions, and International Cat Care warns that a cat rolling over is usually a friendly greeting, not an invitation to touch its tummy. Be careful with the back, flanks, legs and paws too.

What is the best way to pet a cat?

Sit or crouch, offer a hand and let the cat come and rub against it. Stroke the cheeks or ear base for three to five seconds, then stop. If the cat pushes into your hand, carry on; if it doesn't, it may be ready for a break.

Why does my cat follow me but not want to be pet?

Many cats like company without much touch. International Cat Care notes that some cats prefer to sit near you, or even on your lap, without being stroked. Being near is how they choose to be with you.

Why does my cat bite me when I pet it?

Often because the stroking went on longer, harder or in a different place than the cat wanted, and the quieter signals were missed. Shorter sessions, pauses and sticking to the face usually help. If a cat that used to enjoy touch suddenly starts biting, ask your vet.
