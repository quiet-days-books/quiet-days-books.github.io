In short

  • Most cats are busiest around dawn and dusk and sleep 12 to 18 hours a day in many short naps.
  • Cats fit their day around their people: a small 2013 study found cats living closely with their owners were more active in daylight.
  • Predictability, familiarity and routine help cats cope; unusual events, such as a change of carer, upset even healthy cats.
  • A good routine has small meals, short hunting-style play, quiet places to rest and contact the cat chooses.
  • To see your own cat's routine, note where it is and when, for a week, without changing anything.

A cat's daily routine runs on two peaks: busy around dawn and dusk, asleep for much of the rest, about 12 to 18 hours a day in many short naps. Around that it eats small meals, grooms, patrols and watches. A house cat also bends its day around its people, so your routine shapes your cat's.

That last part is why no two cats keep exactly the same day. The general pattern is below, followed by how to see your own cat's version.

What does a cat do all day?

Here is the shape of a typical day for a house cat. Yours will differ, and that difference is the interesting part.

Time What many cats are doing
Around dawn Awake and busy: asking for breakfast, running about, checking the house
Morning Breakfast, a wash, then the first long nap
Middle of the day Mostly sleeping, moving between warm spots as the sun moves
Afternoon Short waking spells: a stretch, a scratch, a look out of the window
Around dusk Lively again: play, chasing, waiting by the kitchen
Evening Company, a patrol of the house, settling near people
Night More sleep, with a few rounds of the house in between

The PDSA describes cats as crepuscular, most active at dusk and dawn, which fits their roots as hunters with eyes adapted to low light. It puts the average sleep at around 15 hours a day, often in several short bursts. Cats Protection gives a range of 12 to 18 hours, with naps from ten minutes to several hours.

Cat morning routine

For many people, the cat's morning routine starts with a paw on the face. Dawn is one of a cat's natural active times, and breakfast tends to follow, so a cat that wakes you early is doing exactly what its clock and its experience suggest.

A typical cat morning goes something like this: wake, stretch, a trip to the food bowl, a check of the windows, a thorough wash, then back to sleep somewhere warm. Our book's activity "The First Place in the Morning" asks you to note only where your cat is the first time you see it each day. Do it for a week and you'll usually find a pattern you never noticed.

If the early wake-ups are a problem, the AAFP and ISFM guidelines suggest puzzle feeders and timed feeders to give small, frequent meals; one timed for dawn can take you out of the loop.

Does my cat know my routine?

Almost certainly. Cats learn what predicts what: the alarm, the kettle, the sound of keys, your footsteps in the hall.

A small 2013 study by Piccione and colleagues tracked the activity of ten pet cats in two households. The cats that lived in closer contact with their owners were more active during daylight than the cats that spent their nights outdoors. The authors concluded that human presence and care strongly influence how active cats are and when. It is a small study, but it matches what many households see: a cat whose busy hours line up with yours.

Cats also know their people by sound alone. In a 2013 experiment, cats that had stopped reacting to strangers calling their name perked up again when they heard their owner's voice. That is one reason a cat may turn up at the door before you have opened it. If your cat shadows you from room to room, our article on why your cat follows you covers what is behind it.

Do cats like routine?

Yes. The AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines put it in one line: predictability, familiarity and routine enhance the cat's coping ability. A cat feels comfortable when it knows its territory and what happens in it.

The other side of this was shown in a 2011 study by Stella, Lord and Buffington. Over 77 weeks, researchers recorded how cats in a research colony reacted to unusual events, such as a change of carer or a disrupted daily schedule. In the weeks with those events, healthy cats as well as cats with a bladder condition were more likely to eat less or go to the toilet outside the litter tray. Cats notice when the day goes off script.

A daily routine that suits most cats

This draws on the AAFP and ISFM guidelines. Fit the times to your own household.

  1. Small meals, some of them hunted. Split the daily food into several portions and serve part of it in a puzzle feeder, or hidden in a few places for the cat to find.
  2. Short play at the cat's busy times. A few minutes with a wand toy around dawn or dusk, moved like a bird or a mouse, ending with the cat catching it.
  3. Fresh water, a clean litter tray, and beds in quiet places. Keep food, water, litter and resting places separate, and leave hiding places alone.
  4. Contact the cat chooses. Let your cat come to you, start the stroking, and walk away when it has had enough. Head and cheeks are usually the welcome spots.
  5. The same order most days. It doesn't need to be to the minute. A predictable order matters more than a precise clock.

For more ways to fill the waking hours, see our guide to things to do with your cat.

When your routine changes

New jobs, holidays, a move and new people all change a cat's day. Keep the things the cat relies on the same where you can: the same food, the same bowls in the same place, the same litter tray and beds. Change other things gradually, and give the cat a quiet room or a high place to retreat to while the house is busy.

Expect a little extra following, some meowing or a few days of sleeping in unusual places. If your cat stops eating, changes its litter habits, hides far more than usual, or the change doesn't settle, ask your vet.

How to see your own cat's routine

The best way to learn your cat's day is to watch it, without steering it. Our book The House Your Cat Knows gives a whole part, Patterns, to this. A few of its activities you can start today:

  • The Time They Arrive. Notice one time your cat tends to turn up near you, and what changes in the room when it does. Don't call it in to prove the pattern.
  • The Nap Rotation. Over a day or a week, write down two or three places your cat rests, and what the light and warmth are like there. Our guide to cat sleeping spots explains what those places tend to mean.
  • The Grooming Hour. Notice where and when your cat usually washes.
  • The Evening Route. Notice where your cat goes as the day turns into evening.
  • One Day in This House. Pick one ordinary day and save a few details as they happen: one place, one sound, one route.

Keep it light. This is about noticing, not tracking. A week of short notes is usually enough to see your cat's day, and it rarely looks quite like anyone else's.

Questions people ask

What is a cat's daily routine?

A typical house cat wakes and gets busy around dawn, eats, grooms, and spends much of the day napping in a rotation of favourite spots. It livens up again around dusk, often patrols the house in the evening, and fits meals and company around when its people are home.

Does my cat know my routine?

Very likely. Cats learn what happens when, and a 2013 study found that cats living closely with their owners shifted more of their activity into daylight. Cats can also recognise their owner's voice.

Do cats like routine?

Yes. Feline welfare guidelines from the AAFP and ISFM say predictability, familiarity and routine help a cat cope. Sudden changes, such as a new carer or a broken daily pattern, can unsettle even healthy cats.

Why does my cat wake me up so early?

Cats are naturally active around dawn, and breakfast usually comes next. If you answer an early meow with food, the cat learns that it works. A puzzle feeder or a small portion left out the night before can take the edge off.

How do I help my cat with a change in routine?

Keep what you can the same: food, bowls, litter tray, beds and the places they live in. Make other changes gradually. If your cat's eating, litter habits or behaviour change and don't settle, ask your vet.
