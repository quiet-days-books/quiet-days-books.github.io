In short

  • A relaxed cat has ears facing forward, soft or half-closed eyes, whiskers out to the side and a loose body, often with the tail up.
  • A worried cat crouches low with its tail tucked, ears turned sideways or flat and pupils wide.
  • An angry or defensive cat may arch its back, raise its fur, flatten its ears and show its teeth. Give it space.
  • Slow blinking is friendly: in a 2020 study, cats narrowed their eyes back at owners who slow-blinked at them.
  • Read the ears, eyes, whiskers, tail and body together, and in context. One signal on its own can mislead.

Cat body language comes from five places at once: the ears, the eyes, the whiskers, the tail and the body. A relaxed cat has ears facing forward, soft eyes, whiskers out to the side and a loose body. A worried cat goes low and tight, with ears turned or flat and pupils wide. An angry cat arches, puffs up and flattens its ears.

Cats Protection's advice is to look at the context as well as the body. The same posture can mean different things in different places.

What do a cat's ears tell you?

Ears are a good first place to look. International Cat Care describes a relaxed cat's ears facing forward in their normal position. Ears that stand up but rotate outward, with narrow pupils, usually signal frustration. Ears held flat against the head with wide pupils are often a sign of fear.

Cats Protection adds finer shades: flat ears for a stressed cat, ears pricked with one turned to the side for an anxious one, and ears turned to the side or back for an unhappy cat.

What do a cat's eyes mean?

Pupils and blinking carry most of the message. A relaxed cat may have eyes half-closed or shut with small pupils, and may blink slowly, according to Cats Protection. Wide eyes with dilated pupils point to a worried cat. International Cat Care adds that a wide-eyed stare with little blinking appears in antagonistic meetings between cats, while a threatened cat may look away or blink rapidly.

The slow blink has been tested. In 2020, Tasmin Humphrey and colleagues at the University of Sussex asked owners of 21 cats, from 14 households, to slow-blink at their cats (18 cat and owner pairs went into the analysis). The cats made more half-blinks and eye narrowing back than when the owner did nothing. In a second experiment with 18 cats, a researcher the cats had never met slow-blinked at them. The cats were more likely to approach the researcher after a slow blink than after a neutral face. The authors read the slow blink as a form of friendly communication between cats and people.

To try it, Cats Protection suggests blinking slowly with your head tilted slightly to the side, and not staring, which a cat can take as a threat. Our article on why your cat stares at you covers the other side of eye contact.

What do a cat's whiskers say?

International Cat Care describes relaxed whiskers pointing straight out to the side and not spread much. When a cat is tense or very alert, its whiskers fan out and point forward. A frightened cat flattens its whiskers against its face and bunches them together.

Forward whiskers alone do not mean trouble. International Cat Care ties them to a cat that is tense or very alert, and an alert cat is not always an upset one.

Cat posture: belly up, crouched and arched

Posture is the part you can read from across the room.

A cat lying on its side or back with its belly showing is usually relaxed. International Cat Care calls rolling over onto the back a social roll, usually a friendly greeting or a sign of play or comfort, and the RSPCA lists a stretched-out cat with its belly exposed as a happy cat. International Cat Care is just as clear that rolling over is not an invitation to touch a cat's tummy. Our guide to the best spot to pet a cat explains where most cats do like to be touched.

A worried cat makes itself small. International Cat Care describes an anxious cat pulling in its head and neck and keeping its body low, with all four paws on the floor. The RSPCA describes the crouch: body held tight, muscles tense, head slightly lowered, tail tucked in.

An angry or defensive cat does the opposite and makes itself big. The RSPCA describes it standing with an arched back, fur raised, tail tense, ears lowered and angled out, teeth showing, and one front paw slightly lifted, ready to swipe. Step back and let it leave.

What does a cat's tail tell you?

Upright usually means hello, tucked means worried, puffed with an arched back means stay away. International Cat Care also notes that a tail held out and swept slowly side to side may mean frustration. Our full guide to cat tail behavior has a chart of tail positions and the research behind the tail-up greeting.

How many facial expressions do cats have?

In a 2023 study, Lauren Scott and Brittany Florkiewicz filmed 53 adult cats at a rescue cat café in Los Angeles and coded their faces with a scientific system built for cats. They counted 276 distinct combinations of facial movements. Of those, 126 appeared only in friendly encounters between cats and 102 only in unfriendly ones.

The friendly and unfriendly faces used different parts. In friendly moments, ears moved forward, whiskers moved forward and eyes closed. In unfriendly ones, ears rotated and flattened against the head, pupils narrowed and the cat licked its lips. The study watched cats with other cats, not with people. Flat ears match what the charities describe. Forward whiskers do not fit so neatly: here they belong to the friendly faces, while International Cat Care reads them as tense or alert, which is one more reason to read the whole cat.

How to read your own cat's body language

Charts give the common readings. Your cat has its own versions, and one way to learn them is to notice what happens just before and just after a signal. Our book The House Your Cat Knows has two activities built around exactly that:

  • "When They Walk Away." Notice one moment when your cat ends an interaction or leaves a place, and write what happened just before and where it went next. The book's one rule: do not follow to continue the interaction.
  • "The Distance They Leave." When your cat settles near someone, record the distance it chooses: one cushion away, across the desk, at your feet. Do not close the gap for a photo.

A week of short notes will show whether your cat repeats anything. If you want more quiet ways to spend that time, our guide to things to do with your cat has ideas, and how to make a cat purr covers a relaxed cat's sound. Sounds are part of the picture too; we cover the late-night kind in why cats meow at night.

A sudden change in how your cat holds itself, moves or reacts to touch can have a physical cause. If something looks different and stays that way, ask your vet.

Questions people ask

How can you tell if a cat is happy by its body language?

Look for ears forward and relaxed, eyes soft, half-closed or blinking slowly, whiskers relaxed and a loose posture, often with the tail held upright. Cats Protection and the RSPCA also list rolling onto the back and lying stretched out with the belly showing as signs of a relaxed, happy cat.

What does it mean when a cat slow blinks at you?

It is a friendly signal. In a 2020 University of Sussex study, cats made more half-blinks and eye narrowing when their owners slow-blinked at them, and were more likely to approach an unfamiliar person who slow-blinked than one with a neutral face. You can slow-blink back.

Does a cat showing its belly want a belly rub?

Usually not. International Cat Care calls rolling onto the back a social roll, usually a friendly greeting or a sign of comfort, and says it is not an invitation to touch the tummy. Leave the tummy alone unless the cat clearly asks for more.

What do flat ears on a cat mean?

Usually fear or stress. International Cat Care says ears held flat against the head with wide pupils are often a sign of fear, and the RSPCA describes an angry or very unhappy cat with ears flattened and pupils dilated. Give the cat room.

What do a cat's whiskers tell you?

According to International Cat Care, relaxed whiskers point out to the side and are less spread, whiskers fanned out and pointing forward mean the cat is tense or very alert, and whiskers flattened against the face and bunched together mean it is frightened.
