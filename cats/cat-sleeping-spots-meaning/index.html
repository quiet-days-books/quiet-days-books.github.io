In short

  • Where a cat sleeps mostly reflects four needs: safety, warmth, a familiar smell and, for many cats, your company.
  • High places and enclosed spaces feel safe; laundry, radiators and sunny windows are about warmth.
  • A cat that sleeps in your spot is choosing your warmth and your scent.
  • Cats rotate their sleeping places on purpose. Moving spots is normal.
  • If your cat suddenly sleeps much more or starts hiding away, ask your vet.

A cat's sleeping spot tells you what it needs most at that moment: safety, warmth, a familiar smell or company. High shelves and boxes are about feeling safe, radiators and laundry about warmth, and your side of the bed about your heat and your scent. Cats also rotate between several spots on purpose, so a move is normal.

Cats sleep a lot, around 12 to 18 hours a day according to Cats Protection, in naps that can be ten minutes or several hours. That's a lot of decisions about where to lie down, and each one says something.

What cat sleeping spots mean

Cats Protection sums up the ideal: somewhere warm, comfortable, elevated and safe. Most spots score high on one or two of those.

Where your cat sleeps What it is usually about
A high shelf, the top of the wardrobe, a cat tree Safety. From above, a cat can watch the room and isn't easy to reach.
A cardboard box, a basket, a drawer Shelter. Small enclosed spaces feel protected and warm up quickly.
Under the bed or behind a chair A refuge. Somewhere quiet with an exit, used when the cat wants less of the household.
A sunny window or the patch of sun on the floor Warmth, and often a view.
On top of laundry, especially fresh from the dryer Warmth, softness and, for worn clothes, a familiar smell.
Above hot pipes, by a radiator, in the airing cupboard Warmth.
Your side of the bed or sofa, your chair Your warmth and your scent.
On you, beside you, by your head or feet Warmth and company.

These are tendencies. The same cat may pick the top of the bookcase in the afternoon and your lap in the evening for different reasons.

Why high and hidden places feel safe

The AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines describe a cat's safe place as private and secure, often raised, giving a sense of enclosure. If a cat can't see a threat coming, it feels safer, even when only part of its body is hidden. A relaxed cat also uses that safe place for rest and sleep.

That's why a box on its side, a perch with a slight dip in the middle, or a carrier left out with a blanket in it can become a favourite bed. VCA Animal Hospitals adds that small spaces warm up quickly, which is a second reason boxes are so popular.

The RSPCA and the guidelines both say cats should always have access to a place to hide. If your cat sleeps somewhere tucked away, the kindest thing is to leave it be: don't reach in, and don't photograph inside.

Cat sleeping in your spot: what it means

You stand up, and within a minute the cat is in your seat. Two things are going on.

The first is warmth. Your spot is the warmest patch of sofa in the room for a while after you leave it.

The second is scent. The guidelines describe how cats use smell and their own scent marks to define the core area where they feel secure. Cats Protection notes that some cats prefer dirty laundry because it smells familiar. Your chair, your pillow and your jumper all carry your smell, which to your cat is part of what home smells like.

So a cat in your spot is choosing a warm, safe-smelling place that's connected to you. If you want the seat back, lift the cat gently or wait; there's no lesson to teach.

Cat sleeping on your bed: by your head, legs or feet

Where your cat lies on the bed often comes down to heat, space and how much contact it likes.

  • Near your head or on the pillow: warm, and you stay put there all night.
  • Between your legs or behind your knees: a sheltered hollow with heat on both sides.
  • At your feet: close, but with room to move and a quick way off the bed.
  • On your chest: maximum warmth and contact. Usually a cat that likes being close.

Among cats that get on, the guidelines list resting or sleeping in contact or close together as one of the signs that they belong to the same social group. When your cat chooses to sleep against you, it is treating you in a similar way.

Some cats never sleep on the bed, and that is no sign of a weaker bond. Our article on why your cat follows you covers the many cats that like to be near you without touching.

Why do cats change sleeping spots?

Cats Protection says it plainly: cats rotate their sleeping places, and it is normal. They link it to wildcat behaviour that may help avoid a build-up of parasites.

There are everyday reasons too:

  • The sun moves. The morning window and the afternoon rug are different spots for the same reason.
  • The seasons change. The warm places in a house in January are not the warm places in July.
  • The house changes. A noisy room, a visitor or a new piece of furniture can send a cat somewhere quieter for a while.

So when your cat abandons the expensive bed for a box, nothing has gone wrong.

Do cats have favourite sleeping spots?

Most cats have a handful of spots they come back to, and one or two that slowly become theirs: a blanket, a box that should have gone out with the recycling, one exact corner of the sofa. That is the "cat favorite spot" people search for, and you probably already know yours.

If you'd like to see the whole set, our book The House Your Cat Knows has an activity called "The Nap Rotation": over a day or a week, write down two or three places your cat rests and what the light, heat and noise are like in each. Another, "The Place That Became Theirs," photographs one claimed place as it is, empty if the cat isn't there, and writes down how it became theirs. Both share one rule: don't wake your cat, use flash or rearrange the light to document sleep.

When a change in sleeping spots is worth a vet call

A new favourite spot is normal. Ask your vet if your cat:

  • is sleeping a lot more than usual,
  • is hiding away more, or avoiding rooms it used to share with you,
  • seems lethargic and has lost interest in food, play or being stroked.

The PDSA notes that a sudden change in sleeping pattern can be a sign of an underlying health condition. The vet is the person to check.

How to map your cat's sleeping spots

You can learn a lot in a week without disturbing a single nap:

  1. Each time you notice your cat asleep, note the place and the time.
  2. Add one detail about the spot: sunny, warm, high, enclosed, near someone.
  3. After a few days, look for the pattern. Most cats have a morning spot, an afternoon spot and an evening spot.
  4. Photograph the empty places, too. A dent in a blanket is a good record.
  5. Leave hiding places out of the photos.

Questions people ask

What does it mean when a cat sleeps in your spot?

Your spot is warm when you leave it, and it smells of you. Cats rely heavily on scent to feel secure in their home, and a familiar smell is part of what makes a place feel safe. Take it as a compliment.

Why do cats change sleeping spots?

Rotating sleeping places is normal cat behaviour. Cats Protection links it to wildcat behaviour that may help keep parasite levels down. Warmth also moves: the sunny patch at 10am is gone by 3pm.

Do cats have favourite sleeping spots?

Yes, most have a few they return to, and they move between them during the day and through the seasons. A blanket, a box or one end of the sofa can become theirs over time.

What does it mean when my cat sleeps near my head?

Usually warmth and closeness. The pillow is warm, and your head stays in the same place all night. Among cats that get on well, resting in contact or close by is a sign of a friendly bond.

Should I worry if my cat starts sleeping somewhere new?

A new spot on its own is normal. If your cat is sleeping a lot more than usual, hiding away more, or avoiding shared rooms, ask your vet.
