In short

  • Colour: cats most likely have two kinds of colour cones, so they see a world close to a person with red-green colour blindness.
  • Dark: cats see about six times better than us in dim light, but not in total darkness.
  • Detail: people resolve roughly four to seven times finer detail; a cat sees sharply at about 20 feet what we see sharply at 100.
  • Movement: a cat's many rod cells pick up small movements, even far away.
  • Cat lenses let through ultraviolet light that ours block, so cats are probably sensitive to some light we cannot see.

Cats see fewer colours than we do, most likely a range close to a person with red-green colour blindness. They see far better in dim light, notice small movements quickly and have a wide view to the sides. What they give up is fine detail: a person resolves several times more of it, so a cat's world looks softer, especially far away.

What colors do cats see?

The answer has changed over the years, and you will still find vet pages that disagree. VCA Animal Hospitals, for example, says cats have three types of cone but about ten times fewer than people, and that scientists disagree about whether cats see only blue and grey or yellow as well.

The most direct test we found points to two. In a 2016 study in Experimental Eye Research, David and Robin Clark noted that most recordings from the cat retina show two daytime receptors, one most sensitive near 460 nm (blue) and one near 560 nm (yellow-green). They then trained two cats to pick coloured light out from white light of varying brightness. Both cats managed it across a range of blues and greens, and both failed at one point, 505 nm, over many trials. That blind spot is almost the same as in people with deuteranopia, a common form of red-green colour blindness. The authors concluded that cats are dichromatic.

So a cat probably sees blues and yellows best and has the most trouble with reds and greens. A red toy on a green rug may stand out to you far more than to your cat. That last point is our reading of the studies, not something anyone tested with toys.

Can cats see in the dark?

Not in complete darkness, but they need much less light than we do. The Merck Veterinary Manual says cats can see about six times better in dim light than people, which is where the myth of seeing in the dark comes from.

Several things help. The retina has many rod cells, which collect dim light. The pupils are elliptical and open very wide, VCA notes, to take in as much light as possible. Behind the retina sits a reflective layer, the tapetum lucidum, which sends light back through the eye for a second chance. It also gives cats' eyes a blue or greenish glint at night; we explain it in why cats' eyes glow.

How sharp is a cat's vision?

Less sharp than ours, but how much less depends on how you test.

Sharpness is measured in cycles per degree: how many pairs of black and white lines an eye can tell apart in one degree of view before they blur to grey. People manage about 60, according to a 2018 review from Duke University that compared some 600 species. The Duke team found humans resolve four to seven times more detail than dogs and cats.

Most behavioural studies put cats at 3 to 9 cycles per degree. In 2013, the Clarks argued that those tests made life hard for the cats, with short time limits and dim targets. Their own two cats, given unlimited time, bright high-contrast stripes and enough distance to focus, reached 15 and 20 cycles per degree. The younger cat with better focus did best. That is still well short of 60, but closer than older figures suggest.

VCA puts it in plain terms: an object a person sees clearly at 100 feet looks blurry to a cat, and only becomes sharp at about 20 feet.

Do cats see well up close?

Not especially. The Merck Veterinary Manual says the lens changes that let an eye focus on near and far objects appear limited in cats. VCA advises that if you want a cat to see you clearly, you stand in front of it, where its sharpest vision is.

Why do cats notice every movement?

VCA notes that the rod cells, which cats have more of than we do, detect motion, even small movements at great distances. The Merck manual adds that cats judge speed and distance very well, which suits a hunter.

A cat may ignore a still toy and pounce the moment it twitches. Moving a wand toy like a small animal running away, rather than holding it up, gives your cat what its eyes are tuned for. More play ideas are in our guide to things to do with your cat.

Movement outside matters too. A cat chattering at a bird it can't reach through the window is reacting to this kind of sight; see why cats chatter.

Can cats see ultraviolet light?

Probably some of it. Our own lenses block ultraviolet light before it reaches the retina. In 2014, Ron Douglas and Glen Jeffery measured how much light gets through the lens and other clear parts of the eye in many mammals. Cats were among the animals, with hedgehogs, dogs, ferrets and okapis, whose lenses let through significant amounts of UVA (315 to 400 nm). The authors concluded these animals are likely to be sensitive to ultraviolet even without a special UV pigment.

Nobody knows what that looks like to a cat. It does mean a cat may notice things in daylight that we cannot see at all.

What your cat sees from where it sits

Put these facts together in your own living room. Your cat sits low, so chair legs, the underside of the table and your feet fill much of its view. It sees the room in muted colours with soft edges, picks out every twitch of movement, and can still find its way when you would reach for a light.

From a high place the view changes again: more of the room, more of what moves through it. A cat staring at you across the room is probably reading your movements more than your face; our article on why your cat stares at you looks at what that attention means.

See the house from their level

Questions people ask

What do cats see?

A world with fewer colours and softer detail than ours, which stays visible in light far too dim for us. Cats pick up small movements well and have a wide view to the sides. Colour vision is probably close to a person with red-green colour blindness.

What colors do cats see?

Most studies of the cat retina find two kinds of colour receptors, one most sensitive near blue (about 460 nm) and one near yellow-green (about 560 nm). In a 2016 test, two cats failed to tell one blue-green wavelength (505 nm) from white, the same blind spot as people with a common form of red-green colour blindness.

Can cats see in the dark?

Not in total darkness. The Merck Veterinary Manual says cats see about six times better than people in dim light. A reflective layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum, sends light back through the eye and gives the green or blue shine you see at night.

Do cats see in black and white?

No. Cats have colour receptors, fewer than we do, and tests show they can tell some colours apart. Their range is narrower than ours, with reds and greens most likely the hardest.

Are cats nearsighted?

Their vision is less sharp than ours at a distance. VCA Animal Hospitals says an object a person sees clearly at 100 feet will not look sharp to a cat until it is about 20 feet away. The Merck Veterinary Manual adds that a cat's ability to change focus appears limited.
