In short

  • Cats' eyes do not make light. A mirror-like layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum, sends light back out, and that is the glow you see.
  • The layer gives the retina a second chance to catch dim light, which is part of why cats see about six times better than us in dim light.
  • The cat's layer is made of cells packed with tiny reflective rods containing zinc and riboflavin; its colour comes from how those rods are stacked.
  • Most cats shine yellow-green or yellow-orange. Cats with blue eyes and pale coats have mostly lost the layer, and the Siamese cats in one 1982 study shone less.
  • The price of the layer is a softer image: a cat sees less fine detail than we do.

Cats' eyes glow because of a reflective layer at the back of the eye, the tapetum lucidum. Light that passes through the retina hits this layer and bounces back, giving the eye a second chance to use it, and part of that light comes back out towards you. The eyes make no light of their own: they return the light from your torch, your headlights or your camera.

What makes a cat's eyes glow?

A layer behind the retina that works like a mirror. The Merck Veterinary Manual describes it as a reflective layer that magnifies incoming light and gives cats' eyes their blue or greenish glint at night.

Here is the order of events. Light enters through the pupil and passes through the retina, where the light-sensitive cells sit. Some of it is caught on the way in; the rest would normally be lost. In a cat, it hits the tapetum lucidum and is sent straight back through the retina, so the same cells get a second try. A 2004 comparative study in Veterinary Ophthalmology sums up its job in those terms: more sensitivity at low light.

Whatever the retina still does not catch on the way back leaves the eye. That is the shine. Cats Protection lists the usual moments people notice it: car headlights and camera flash in the dark.

What is the tapetum lucidum made of?

In cats it is built of cells, a kind anatomists call a tapetum cellulosum. A 1990 electron microscope study placed it in the choroid, behind the retina, in the upper part of the back of the eye.

The cells hold tiny rods. A 1981 lab study found zinc in them, bound to the amino acid cysteine as zinc cysteinate (Kohler). Braidee Foote, a veterinary ophthalmology professor at the University of Tennessee, names the reflective compound in cats as riboflavin, a B vitamin.

You will often read that the amount of zinc decides the colour of the glow. We found no study that tests that directly. What has been measured is the structure, below.

Why do cats' eyes glow different colours?

Mostly because of how the layer is built. In 1971, the physiologist J. A. Coles looked at fresh cat tapetum under a microscope and found it made of tiny stacked reflectors, each less than 6 micrometres across. He shone white light on single ones and watched the colour that came back. Reflectors near the surface tended to send back blue light, and deeper ones longer wavelengths, because their layers were spaced differently.

Coles put the colours down to the spacing of the layers inside each reflector.

Foote writes that in cats the glow is most often yellow-green or yellow-orange, and that the colour varies, just like irises do.

Why do some cats' eyes glow red, or hardly at all?

Some cats have less of the layer, or a weaker one.

Foote notes that animals with blue eyes and white or light-coloured coats have mostly lost the tapetum. Dr Lizzie Youens, a vet writing for Cats.com, says albino and blue-eyed cats give a red-eye effect instead of the usual shine.

Siamese cats are a measured case. A 1982 study examined nine Siamese cats and found their tapetum reflected less light than that of ordinary cats. Under the electron microscope, four of the nine had many tapetal cells filled with disrupted rods, and the rods held significantly less zinc than in ordinary cats. The authors suggested a hereditary disorder of relatively high frequency.

Why do cats have a tapetum lucidum?

For seeing in dim light. The Merck manual says cats see about six times better than people in dim light. Cats Protection gives six to eight times; Foote says cats can see in light only 16% as bright as what people need. The reflective layer is one part of that, together with the many rod cells in the retina and pupils that open very wide, up to 50% larger than ours according to Foote.

Many other animals have one. The 2004 comparative study lists carnivores, rodents and whales and dolphins among animals with the cellular kind cats have, and cows, sheep, goats and horses with a fibrous kind. Pigs, squirrels, birds, the red kangaroo and primates have none, and the authors note these are usually animals active by day. Humans are primates, so we have none either.

Road studs are named after this glow. Popular Science tells how the businessman Percy Shaw, driving in Yorkshire on a foggy night in 1933, saw two bright dots flash back from the roadside. They belonged to a cat. Within a year he had invented Catseye reflectors, the studs still set into roads.

Does the glow mean cats see in the dark?

No. The glow only shows there was light to reflect. Cats Protection is plain about it: like us, cats cannot see when there is absolutely no light. What they can do is make much more of a little light, which is why a cat moves easily through a room you would call dark.

There is a cost. Foote explains that all the light bouncing around off the tapetum can make what a cat sees a little fuzzier, so animals with a tapetum give up some visual acuity for dim-light vision. A cat needs to be about seven times closer to something than a person to see it as sharply in bright light, she says. Our article on what cats see goes through colour, detail and movement in full.

Good dim-light vision also explains part of a cat's evening and night life. See why cats meow at night and why cats sleep so much.

How to photograph your cat without glowing eyes

The shine needs two things: a light coming from close to where you stand, and wide pupils letting it in. What follows is our reasoning from how the layer works, not a tested method, so try it and see.

  1. Turn the flash off. A flash fires from right beside the lens, and Cats Protection names camera flash as one of the usual causes of the glow.
  2. Turn the room lights up instead. The Merck manual explains that a cat's pupil gets smaller in bright surroundings, so less light reaches the layer.
  3. Shoot from a little to the side rather than straight on.
  4. Use daylight from a window when you can.

When the house turns to evening

The glow is an evening thing: lights going on, a cat crossing the hall, two bright discs from under a chair. Our book The House Your Cat Knows has an activity for exactly that hour. Questions people ask

Why do cats' eyes glow in the dark?

They reflect light. Behind the retina, cats have a reflective layer called the tapetum lucidum. Light that passes through the retina without being caught bounces off it and goes back through for a second chance; some of it leaves the eye, and that is the glow you see in headlights, torchlight or a camera flash.

Why do cats' eyes glow green?

Green and yellow-green are the most common colours, but the shine can also look yellow-orange or blue. In 1971 a physiologist found the cat tapetum is made of tiny layered reflectors: those near the surface reflect blue light and deeper ones longer wavelengths, depending on the spacing of their layers.

Do all cats' eyes glow?

Most do. A veterinary ophthalmologist writing in The Conversation notes that animals with blue eyes and white or light-coloured coats have mostly lost the layer, and a 1982 study of nine Siamese cats found their tapetum reflected less light than that of ordinary cats.

Do cats' eyes glow without any light?

No. The glow is reflected light, so in complete darkness there is nothing to reflect. Cats Protection also points out that cats cannot see when there is absolutely no light.

Why don't human eyes glow like cats' eyes?

People have no tapetum lucidum. Neither do pigs, squirrels, birds, the red kangaroo or other primates. A 2004 comparative study notes that animals without it are usually active by day.
