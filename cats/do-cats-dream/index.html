Cats almost certainly dream. They have REM sleep, the stage in which people who are woken almost always remember a dream, and when the scientist Michel Jouvet removed the muscle paralysis of that stage, sleeping cats got up and stalked, pounced and groomed. What a cat actually sees in its sleep is something no one can know.

That gap between what has been shown and what can only be guessed is the interesting part. Here is where the line sits.

Do cats dream? What the evidence says

Sleep in mammals has two main kinds: slow wave sleep, and REM sleep, also called paradoxical sleep. Jouvet named it "paradoxical sleep" in 1959, according to a 2018 tribute by the neuroscientist Barbara Jones.

In people, the link between REM sleep and dreaming is strong. Jouvet wrote in 1979 that a person woken during this stage will almost always describe a vivid dream. Cats have the same stage. So the reasoning goes: same brain state, very likely a similar experience.

The best evidence comes from what happens when the normal brakes of REM sleep are taken off.

Michel Jouvet's sleeping cats

During REM sleep the body's muscles go slack. This is called atonia, and it is why a sleeping cat stays mostly flat even when its whiskers and ears twitch. Jouvet wrote that during ordinary REM sleep "a dreaming cat lies totally flat on the ground".

In 1965 Jouvet and Delorme found that small lesions in a part of the brainstem called the pons removed this paralysis without stopping REM sleep itself. A 2022 review in the Journal of Sleep Research notes that this gave a cat model of what is now called REM sleep behaviour disorder, twenty years before the disorder was identified in humans.

In his 1979 review, titled "What does a cat dream about?", Jouvet described what those cats did. At the moment they entered REM sleep, they raised their heads and moved them as if watching something. Their pupils stayed narrow, and they did not follow moving objects placed in front of them. Then came sequences in no fixed order:

What the sleeping cat did Jouvet's description, in short
Watching Head raised and moving, as if following something
Stalking Walked around the cage as if pursuing prey, sometimes stopped with one front leg raised
Attack Play-like pawing or cuffing, sometimes biting at nothing
Flight Semi-crouched posture, as if escaping
Rage Arched back and fur standing up
Grooming Licking the forelegs or the cage floor

Each run could last up to about five minutes, the average length of a REM episode. In more than 300 episodes recorded on video, Jouvet never saw sexual behaviour. And some cats that were always very friendly when awake showed a lot of aggressive behaviour in their sleep.

What do cats dream of?

The short honest answer: nobody knows. Jouvet himself wrote that, in people, what the dreamer reports on waking is still the only way to explore the content of dreams. A cat cannot report anything.

What the experiments show is that the brain, during REM sleep, runs movement programmes for ordinary cat behaviour: watching, hunting, defending, cleaning. Jouvet called this "oneiric behaviour", meaning dream behaviour, and treated it as a class of innate behaviour. That makes it reasonable to think a cat's dreams are made of cat things. It does not tell us whether there is a mouse in them.

The closest anyone has come to reading dream content in animals was in rats, not cats. In 2001, Matthew Wilson and Kenway Louie at MIT recorded brain cells in rats that had run a track. In about half of more than 40 REM episodes, the cells repeated the pattern they had produced during the run, at about the same speed. Wilson took this as evidence the rats were dreaming about real experiences. Nobody has done the same with cats.

Do cats dream about their owners?

There is no evidence either way. Jouvet's cats acted out hunting, fighting, fleeing and grooming, which are things a body can show. A person in a dream would leave no visible trace, so the question can't be tested with the methods used so far.

What we do know is that a cat that sleeps near you chooses that spot. Our article on cat sleeping spots meaning looks at what the choice of place can tell you.

How much do cats dream?

Cats Protection says domestic cats sleep around 12 to 18 hours a day, and the PDSA gives an average of about 15 hours. Not all of that is REM sleep.

In a table of sleep in 51 mammals from Jerome Siegel's lab at UCLA, published with a 2018 paper in Current Biology and counting only species where REM was measured with brain recordings, the cat row reads 12.5 hours of total sleep, of which 3.2 hours are REM and 9.3 hours non-REM.

Species Total sleep (hours a day) REM sleep (hours a day)
Cat 12.5 3.2
Dog 10.1 2.9
Human 8 1.9

So if REM sleep is when cats dream, a cat may spend around three hours a day in that state, spread over many short episodes. For the bigger picture of why cats sleep so many hours, see why do cats sleep so much.

Twitching paws and whiskers: is my cat dreaming?

Probably. Cats Protection says that when a cat is deeply asleep you might notice its mouth or paws twitching, likely because it is dreaming, and that this is completely normal. Jouvet listed twitching of the whiskers and ears among the small movements that happen during REM sleep while the rest of the body stays still.

Cats Protection also notes that a cat that looks asleep is often still alert to its surroundings, with ears moving to catch sounds. Twitching ears alone don't tell you which kind of sleep you are seeing; a body that is completely loose, with small twitches of the paws, mouth and whiskers, fits REM sleep best.

How to watch your cat dream without waking it

  1. Stay where you are. Cats Protection says cats prefer to sleep in a warm, comfortable, safe place, and a looming person changes that.
  2. Don't touch or call the cat to "help" it. The twitching is normal.
  3. Keep the room as it is: lights, sounds, doors.
  4. If you want a record, a short video from a distance is enough. Turn off the camera sound and flash.
  5. Note the time and the spot. Over a few weeks you will see where your cat sleeps most deeply.

Questions people ask

Do cats dream?

Very likely. Cats have REM, or paradoxical, sleep, the stage in which people who are woken almost always report a dream. When Michel Jouvet removed the muscle paralysis of this stage, sleeping cats moved as if hunting or grooming. What they experience cannot be measured, so the honest answer is very likely, not certain.

What do cats dream of?

Nobody knows. In Jouvet's experiments, cats whose REM paralysis was removed stalked, pounced, groomed, fled and showed rage while asleep, all ordinary cat behaviour. That suggests their dreams involve everyday cat activity, but Jouvet himself noted that dream content can only be explored through what the dreamer reports.

Do cats dream about their owners?

There is no evidence either way, and there may never be. Researchers can see what a sleeping cat's body does, not what it pictures. Jouvet's cats acted out hunting, grooming, fleeing and fighting. People did not appear in that record, but nobody could have seen them if they were there.

Do cats dream when they sleep?

Not all the time. Sleep has stages, and dreaming is linked to REM sleep. In a table of mammal sleep from Jerome Siegel's lab, cats sleep about 12.5 hours a day, of which about 3.2 hours are REM. The rest, about 9.3 hours, is non-REM sleep.

Should I wake my cat if it twitches in its sleep?

No. Cats Protection says twitching mouth or paws in deep sleep is likely dreaming and completely normal. Let the cat sleep and watch from where you are. There is nothing you need to do about it.
