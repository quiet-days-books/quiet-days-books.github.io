In short

  • Pet cats live around 12 to 14 years on average, International Cat Care says, and late teens or early twenties are not unusual.
  • No study has measured indoor-only cats as a separate group, so figures like '15 to 20 years indoors, 2 to 5 outdoors' are guesses.
  • What studies do show: in young cats with outdoor access, road traffic accidents are the most common cause of death.
  • Neutering, a healthy weight and being female or crossbred go with longer lives in UK vet records.
  • An indoor cat still needs room to play, climb and perch, or the vets' guidelines warn of weight gain and stress.

Indoor cats usually live into their early or mid teens, and late teens or early twenties are not unusual. International Cat Care puts the average for pet cats at around 12 to 14 years. A study of 4,009 cats seen by English vets found a median of 14 years. No study has measured indoor-only cats on their own, so a confident figure like "15 to 20 years indoors" is an estimate, not a measurement.

Below is what has been measured, what has not, and what an indoor life needs to go the distance.

How long do indoor cats live on average?

The best numbers come from vet records, and they cover pet cats in general, whether or not they go outside.

Two different measures sit in that table. A median of 14 says half the cats lived longer than 14 years. A life expectancy of 11.74 is an average from birth, and the cats that died young pull it down. Neither is the age your cat "should" reach.

The oldest cat on record, Creme Puff from Texas, lived to 38 years and 3 days, Cats Protection says. In the English study the oldest cat was 26.7.

Do indoor cats live longer than outdoor cats?

Cats Protection says there is limited evidence that either lifestyle leads to a longer lifespan. Nobody has measured the overall difference, though one risk is clear.

The clearest evidence is about traffic. The Bristol Cats study followed 2,190 pet cats in the UK from when they were young until age 8. About 81% were still alive at 8. Of the cats that died, road traffic accidents were the most common cause, 45.6% of deaths, and in cats aged 1 to 6 the share was 49.6%. The authors note that restricting cats indoors can reduce that risk, and add that an indoor-only life may not meet all of a cat's behavioural needs.

In the English vet-record study, trauma was the most common cause of death across all ages, at 12.2%.

What about "outdoor cats live 2 to 5 years"?

We looked for a study behind this number and found none. PetMD, which we opened, says outdoor cats live about half as long as indoor cats, with no source for the figure. The UK studies above include many cats that go outside, and their averages sit between 11.7 and 14 years, far above five.

So the honest version is shorter. Keeping a cat in removes the traffic risk, which matters most in young cats. Whether that adds years to a typical cat's life, and how many, has not been measured.

What else changes how long a cat lives?

The UK studies looked at sex, neutering, breed and body weight. They did not split cats by indoor or outdoor life.

Teng and colleagues found that being entire, purebred and at a non-ideal body weight were all linked to a shorter life. Weight is the one that an indoor life can push the wrong way.

What an indoor life takes away, according to vets

The 2021 AAHA/AAFP life stage guidelines set it out both ways. An indoor-only lifestyle may lower the risks of injury, predators, poisoning and infection. It may also raise the risks of compromised welfare, illness, obesity and behaviour problems because of environmental limits. The guidelines call environmental enrichment essential for indoor cats.

The AAFP's 2019 position statement on indoor cats goes further: several behaviour problems and medical conditions seen in cats come from stress caused by an unsuitable indoor environment. Meeting a cat's needs, it says, reduces that stress.

The guidelines also mention a middle way. Supervised outdoor time, on a harness or in a cat-proof enclosure, may reduce some of the risks of going outside. Our guide to things to do with your cat covers safe time outdoors and play at home.

How to give an indoor cat a long, full life

The AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines say predatory behaviour happens even in well-fed cats, and a cat with no outlet for it can end up overweight, bored or frustrated. Here is what they and Cats Protection suggest:

  1. Play with a wand toy. Move it like flying or running prey, and let the cat catch it at the end.
  2. Make some food a hunt: a ball or puzzle feeder the cat has to work.
  3. Give it a perch and a few high places to climb.
  4. Offer toys the cat can handle with its paws or mouth, including ones that hold food.
  5. Keep up with vet checks. Cats Protection suggests at least once a year; the AAHA/AAFP guidelines say cats over 10 should be seen at least every six months. Your vet will tell you what fits your cat.
  6. If your cat goes out, keep it in at night, as Cats Protection advises.

How old is my indoor cat in life stages?

The 2021 AAHA/AAFP guidelines use four age stages: up to 1 year, young adult from 1 to 6 years, mature adult from 7 to 10, and senior over 10.

For a human-age comparison, see our chart of cat years to human years, or put in your cat's age in the cat age calculator.

The same guidelines note that owners of older cats often describe small changes such as "not getting on the counters as much" or a cat that no longer likes its window seat. Those can be signs of stiff joints rather than a change of mind. Mention them at the next vet visit.

Keep a record of an ordinary day

Numbers describe thousands of cats. Your cat has one particular way of filling a day in your home: where it sleeps after breakfast, which chair it takes at night, the spot it chooses when the heating comes on.

Our book The House Your Cat Knows ends with an activity called One Day in This House. You choose a day you are already living, not a special one, and keep a few notes on how your cat moves through it. Another, The Nap Rotation, asks you to write down two or three places your cat rests over a day or a week and what the light, heat or noise is like in each. Neither asks your cat to do anything new.

Questions people ask

How long do indoor cats live?

Most live into their early or mid teens, and many go further. International Cat Care puts the average for pet cats at around 12 to 14 years, with late teens or early twenties not unusual. A study of 4,009 cats in English vet practices found a median of 14 years. No study has timed indoor-only cats separately.

Do indoor cats live longer than outdoor cats?

They avoid the main danger for young cats outdoors: in a UK study following cats to age 8, road traffic accidents caused 45.6% of deaths. But Cats Protection says there is limited evidence that either lifestyle leads to a longer life, and the vets' guidelines say an indoor-only life brings its own risks, such as weight gain and stress, when a cat's needs are not met.

Is it true outdoor cats only live 2 to 5 years?

We could not find a study behind that number. The pages that repeat it do not cite one. The large UK studies of pet cats, many of which go outside, found averages of 11.7 to 14 years.

How old is the oldest cat?

Creme Puff, a cat from Texas, lived to 38 years and 3 days, according to Cats Protection. That is far beyond the usual span. In a UK study of vet records the oldest cat had reached 26.7 years.

At what age is an indoor cat a senior?

The 2021 AAHA/AAFP guidelines call cats over 10 years old senior, after young adult (1 to 6 years) and mature adult (7 to 10). Senior cats should see the vet at least every six months, the same guidelines say. Ask your vet what suits your cat.
